## About the Curriculum

Most children experience "the law" simply as a list of things they are not allowed to do, enforced by authority figures. This curriculum reframes the legal system as the operating system of human society. Students learn that laws are just codified rules designed to prevent system crashes (chaos), contracts are just API specifications for human promises, and courts are diagnostic tools for edge cases.

This is an educational curriculum, **not legal advice** — real legal problems need a trusted adult or qualified professional.

## Core Concepts

These mental models help students transition from seeing rules as arbitrary restrictions to viewing them as structural engineering for group behavior.

- **The Rule of Law is System Stability:** In a functional system, the code applies equally to all nodes. We contrast the "Rule of Law" (predictable, objective) with the "Rule of Man" (arbitrary, dependent on the mood of the person in charge), showing how predictability allows societies to scale.
- **Contracts are Interface Specifications:** A contract isn't just a piece of paper; it's the anatomy of a promise. We break down the mechanics of a binding agreement: Offer (the input), Acceptance (the handshake), and Consideration (the payload/value exchanged).
- **Precedent is Version Control:** You can't rewrite the entire codebase every time there is a dispute. Students learn how courts look at past decisions (precedent) to patch bugs and update the law incrementally, ensuring forward compatibility.
- **The Letter vs. The Intent:** A rule might explicitly state one thing, but be designed to prevent something else. Students explore the friction between what a rule says literally (the letter) and what the system actually needs (the intent) to identify loopholes and bad incentives.
- **Rights are System Firewalls:** We strip away the abstract political rhetoric around "rights" and treat them as hardcoded boundaries. A right is a constraint on institutional power, preventing the network (the government or majority) from overriding the individual node.

## The Curriculum Roadmap

This 18-week core progression, with optional advanced weeks, moves from the theoretical need for rules to the practical application of writing, interpreting, testing, and revising them.

### Weeks 1–3: The Architecture of Rules

**Focus:** Chaos, Order, and the Baseline Need for Law
We start at zero. Why do we even need rules? We explore the tragedy of the commons, the concept of private property as an arbitrary but necessary boundary, and how laws reduce the friction of daily survival.

- **Key Activity: The Island System Boot** — Students are given a scenario with zero rules and limited resources. They must identify the exact moment a rule becomes structurally necessary to prevent a system collapse, and draft the first three laws of their society.

### Weeks 4–7: The Anatomy of a Promise (Contracts)

**Focus:** Micro-Law, Accountability, and Consideration
We scale down from society to the individual. How do two people make an enforceable deal? We look at what happens when promises lack "consideration" (an exchange of value) and how vague language creates systemic failure in a transaction.

- **Key Activity: The Chores API** — Students draft a clear household agreement that borrows contract design tools: offer, acceptance, consideration, definitions, edge cases, and remedies. The goal is precision and fairness, not pretending every household agreement is automatically court-enforceable.

### Weeks 8–10: Debugging the Code (Interpretation)

**Focus:** Intent vs. Text, Loopholes, and Precedent
No rule can account for every variable. We look at the famous legal thought experiment: "No vehicles in the park." Does that include bicycles? Wheelchairs? An ambulance? A decommissioned tank used as a memorial?

- **Key Activity: The Precedent Protocol** — Students act as a judge for a series of escalating edge cases violating a poorly written rule. Each decision they make becomes "precedent" that they must legally abide by in the next, slightly different case, forcing them to see how early interpretations lock in future outcomes.

### Weeks 11–14: The Governance Project

**Focus:** Distributed Power, Rights, and Application
We pull the concepts together by looking at how a system governs itself without creating a dictatorship. We look at distributed power (checks and balances) as a way to prevent a single point of failure.

- **Key Activity: The Micro-Charter** — Students identify a shared domain (a Minecraft server, a neighborhood friend group, or a shared bedroom) and write a foundational Charter. It must include three explicit rules, two unalienable "rights" for the users, and a specific dispute-resolution protocol (a micro-court system) for when things go wrong. They then stress-test the document against hypothetical conflicts.

### Weeks 15–18: The Justice System in Action

**Focus:** Courts, Due Process, and Mock Proceedings
Students learn how disputes are resolved through formal legal proceedings, experiencing the full arc from filing a complaint to delivering a verdict in a mock trial.

## How to Use It

You do not need to run all 18 weeks. Every lesson stands alone as a 10–20 minute discussion or activity — use one lesson, one arc, or the full sequence.

- **New to Literacy for Kids?** The hub's [Start Here guide](https://www.literacy-for-kids.com/docs/start-here/) has dedicated pages [for parents](https://www.literacy-for-kids.com/docs/start-here/parents/) and [for teachers and facilitators](https://www.literacy-for-kids.com/docs/start-here/facilitators/).
- **Only have 20 minutes?** Pick any week on the [curriculum site](https://legal.literacy-for-kids.com/), read the big idea, run one activity, and ask one discussion question.
- **Want grab-and-go materials?** The [Shared Toolkit Printables](https://www.literacy-for-kids.com/docs/toolkits/printables/) are one-page cards kids can keep nearby.

## Shared Skills

This curriculum connects to the seven cross-curriculum [Shared Toolkits](https://www.literacy-for-kids.com/docs/toolkits/) — short life-skill modules that support every literacy: **Coping Skills, Communication, Problem Solving, Learning How to Learn, Executive Function, Collaboration, and Information Organization.**

The curriculum site includes a local doorway page for each toolkit (connecting the skill to rules, rights, and agreements specifically), and weekly lessons contain "Moment" callouts that surface the right skill at the right time — like sorting facts from rules from questions, or disagreeing without attacking during a mock trial.
