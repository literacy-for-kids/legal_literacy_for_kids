Week 12: Distributed Power and Single Points of Failure

The Governance Project — Phase 4

If your entire house ran on a single light switch, you'd have a problem.

When that switch broke, every light would go out. There'd be no backup. No alternative. The whole system would depend on one component working perfectly forever — which is something almost no component does.

This is called a single point of failure, and in engineering, it's a sign of bad design.

Governance has the same problem. If one person — a king, a CEO, a principal, a parent — has all the power, then everything depends on that one person. When they're wise, fair, and well-rested, the system runs fine. When they're tired, angry, biased, or simply mistaken, the whole system pays the price.

This week we explore distributed power — the principle that important decisions should require more than one source of authority. Or in software terms: fault tolerance through redundancy.

Facilitator Snapshot
  • This week's idea: don't put all the power in one place. Distribute it so that no single mistake or bias takes down the whole system.
  • The classic version of this idea is separation of powers (legislative, executive, judicial) — but the underlying principle applies to any system.
  • The student should leave thinking about whether their Micro-Charter has any single points of failure.
  • Use household examples wherever possible — they land better than civics-textbook examples.

Prep time~10 minutes

Key vocabularydistributed power, single point of failure, checks and balances, separation of powers, fault tolerance
DifficultyAdvanced

Facilitator Preparation​

Before You Begin
  • Have the student's Case Notes handy — we'll be referencing their candidate Micro-Charter rights from Week 11.
  • Prepare a real example of a household decision that's currently centralized (one parent decides everything about a particular topic) and one that's distributed (multiple people have to agree).
Facilitation Mindset

Distributed power often slows things down — that's not a bug, that's the feature. Help the student see that the goal isn't speed; it's avoiding catastrophic mistakes that come from concentrated authority.

For Younger Learners (Ages 8–9)​

Adapting This Week

Simplest version of the concept: "If only one person can make a decision, and they're wrong, the whole system is wrong. If two or three people have to agree, mistakes get caught."

What to shorten or skip:

  • Skip the "separation of powers" terminology.
  • Skip the formal legislative/executive/judicial breakdown — focus on household examples.
  • Keep sessions to 20 minutes.

Adapting the activities:

  • Use the "one switch / many switches" analogy literally with drawings.
  • Accept oral answers.

Journal alternative: Draw a house with one light switch (and a kid in the dark) vs. a house with many switches.

What success looks like: The learner can name one decision in their life that has a single point of failure, and propose a way to distribute it.

For Ages 10–12
  • Introduce the classical three-branch separation of powers (legislative makes the laws, executive enforces them, judicial interprets them) and discuss why those three functions are separated.
  • Discuss what happens when one branch tries to take over the role of another. (Historical examples without political fights — Watergate, Caesar crossing the Rubicon.)
  • Explore the trade-off: distributed power slows things down. When is that a feature, and when is it a problem?

Guided Session 1​

Single Point of Failure​

Learning Goal​

By the end of this session, the student can:

  • recognize a single point of failure in a real-world system
  • explain why concentrated power is structurally fragile
  • name systems they participate in that have single points of failure

Activities​

1. The One-Switch House

Set up the analogy:

"Imagine a house where every single light, appliance, and door lock runs off one big switch. When that switch is on, everything works. When it's off, nothing works. When the switch breaks, the whole house goes dark."

"What would you do to fix this design?"

The student usually says: have many switches. Each light gets its own. Or at least: have backup switches that can take over.

Drop the term:

"That single switch is what engineers call a single point of failure. When the whole system depends on one component, the whole system can break for one reason. Adding more components — redundancy — makes the system more fault-tolerant."

2. Single Points of Failure in Governance

Now the analogy gets political. Walk through these scenarios:

"Imagine a system where one person — a king, a principal, a CEO — has total authority. They write the rules, decide who's broken them, and decide the punishment. What could go wrong?"

Brainstorm:

  • They could be biased.
  • They could be tired and grumpy on a given day.
  • They could be wrong about the facts.
  • They could be corrupt or self-interested.
  • They could be confused about what the rule was for.
  • They could be incapacitated (sick, missing, dead).
  • They could be threatened or pressured.

Notice: it's not that the person is bad. It's that any single person, no matter how good, is one component. Components fail. Good design doesn't depend on perfect components.

3. Single Points in Real Life

Identify single points of failure in the student's actual life:

SystemWho has the power?What happens if they're wrong / absent / biased?
Family dinner menu(Whoever cooks)Bad meal, or no meal
Class behavior rulesThe teacherInconsistent enforcement
Sibling disputes(Whoever's loudest, or the parent)Unfair outcomes
Game night choice(Whoever speaks up first)Bored players

For each, ask: would this system be better if more than one person had to agree?

Guided Session 2​

Designing Distributed Power​

Learning Goal​

By the end of this session, the student can:

  • describe how distributed power prevents single-point failures
  • identify the three classical branches of government and what each does
  • propose distributed-power structures for their Micro-Charter

Activities​

1. The Three-Branch Pattern

Most modern governments use a specific distributed-power design. Three separate branches, each with a different job:

BranchJobAnalogy
LegislativeWrites the rulesThe team that designs the code
ExecutiveEnforces the rulesThe team that runs the program
JudicialInterprets the rules in specific casesThe team that debugs problems

Why three? Because:

  • The rule-makers shouldn't also enforce, or they'll just enforce against whoever they don't like.
  • The enforcers shouldn't also write the rules, or they'll write rules that maximize their own power.
  • The interpreters shouldn't be the same as either group, or they'll just protect their friends.

Each branch can also check the other two. The legislative can rewrite a law if the judicial interprets it weirdly. The judicial can rule that the executive overstepped. The executive can decline to enforce a law that seems wrong (in some systems).

This is what people mean by checks and balances — each branch can correct the others when they go wrong.

2. The Household Version

Walk through how a household could use three-branch thinking:

"Imagine a household. Right now, maybe one parent makes all the rules, enforces all the rules, and decides what counts as a violation. That's one person doing three jobs."

"What if instead: family meetings make the rules (legislative). Whoever's around enforces them in the moment (executive). When there's a dispute about whether someone really broke a rule, a different person — maybe the OTHER parent, or a sibling council — decides (judicial)."

"Would that be better? Worse? Slower? Fairer?"

It would probably be slower. But it would also be more consistent and harder to abuse. Most households don't fully separate these roles — but noticing that they're three different jobs is the start of seeing where things could go wrong.

3. Micro-Charter Power Design

Pull out the student's notes on their Micro-Charter (started in Week 11). Now ask:

"In your domain, who currently has the power to (a) make rules, (b) enforce them, and (c) decide if someone broke them?"

For example, if their domain is a friend group:

  • Rule-making: often, the most assertive friend
  • Enforcement: whoever's mad in the moment
  • Interpretation: usually no one — disputes just escalate

Now ask:

"If you were designing your Charter to distribute these three powers, how would you do it?"

Possible answers:

  • Rules made by full-group vote (not just one person)
  • Enforcement by a rotating role ("this week, X is the moderator")
  • Disputes resolved by a person who isn't involved (or by group discussion)

The student adds these power-distribution ideas to their Micro-Charter notes.

Independent Practice​

Goal​

Audit the power distribution in the student's actual domains.

Activities​

1. The Power Audit

Pick your Micro-Charter domain (the one you committed to last week). Map out:

  • Who currently makes the rules?
  • Who currently enforces them?
  • Who currently decides disputes?

For each, ask:

  • Is this one person, or distributed?
  • What happens when this person is absent, tired, biased, or wrong?
  • Would distributing this power make things better, or just slower?

2. The Single-Point Test

Find one single point of failure in your real life — a place where one person's decision can take the whole system down. Examples:

  • The one sibling who controls the WiFi password
  • The one friend who decides where the group meets up
  • The one parent who decides what's for dinner
  • The one teacher whose mood determines whether the class has a good day

Write:

  • What the single point of failure is.
  • One way to introduce distributed power.
  • Why the current design might still be in place anyway (sometimes single points are fast, even though they're fragile).

  • "If they were wrong, ___ would happen."
  • "A fairer way would be ___."

Reflection Questions​

  • When you've experienced unfair decisions in your life, was it usually because one person had too much power? Or because the system worked, but you didn't agree with the outcome?
  • Are there situations where you actually want a single point of failure? (Hint: when you need fast decisions — emergencies, etc.)
  • Why do you think most foundational documents — like national constitutions — go to so much trouble to distribute power?

Check for Understanding​

After this week, check whether the learner can:

  1. Define a single point of failure: "What's a single point of failure?" (Looking for: when one component's failure breaks the whole system.)
  2. Name the three branches: "What are the three classical branches of government and what does each one do?" (Looking for: legislative writes, executive enforces, judicial interprets.)
  3. Spot one in real life: "Tell me about a system in your life with too much power concentrated in one person." (Looking for: any honest example.)

If the learner can do at least 2 of these, they're ready for Week 13.

Pause and Notice​

What Matters Here

After Session 2, ask:

"Distributing power means decisions take longer. Sometimes a lot longer. Is that worth it?"

This is a real trade-off, and reasonable people disagree about it. Distributed systems are slower, more frustrating, and full of compromises. Centralized systems are faster, cleaner, and often more decisive — but they also fail more dramatically when the center makes a mistake.

"In the moment, when you really want something, do you wish the system was distributed or centralized? What about when someone else really wants something you disagree with?"

Most people want centralized power when they like the person in charge, and distributed power when they don't. That's actually a useful test: a fair distributed system should work even when you are in the minority.

This week's takeaway: Concentrated power is fast, clean, and dangerous. Distributed power is slow, messy, and resilient. Most well-designed systems choose resilience over speed when the stakes are high — because the system has to keep working even when the person in charge is wrong.

Spiral Review​

Connecting to Earlier Weeks

From Week 11: Rights are firewalls that limit what any one branch can do to an individual. Distributed power is the broader principle that limits what any one branch can do, period.

From Week 3: When you wrote your Three Laws for the island, where did the power live? Was it distributed? Look back. If not, would you redistribute it now?

From Week 10: Even the judicial branch is constrained by precedent. Notice that no single power in a well-designed system is fully unchecked.

Simplify (Ages 8–9)

Stick with the one-light-switch / many-light-switches analogy. Skip the three branches of government. Focus on "many people deciding together is safer than one person deciding alone."

Extend (Ages 10–12)

Look at how different countries distribute power differently. Most democracies have three branches, but they configure them very differently (parliamentary vs. presidential systems, judicial review or not, etc.). Discuss: is there a "best" configuration, or do different configurations work better in different contexts?

Vocabulary This Week
  • Single point of failure — A component whose failure causes the whole system to fail.
  • Distributed power — Authority spread across multiple sources so no single one can override the system.
  • Checks and balances — A design where each part of the system can correct mistakes in the other parts.
  • Separation of powers — Specifically, separating rule-making, enforcement, and interpretation into different bodies.
  • Fault tolerance — A system's ability to keep working even when some parts fail.

Preview of Next Week​
