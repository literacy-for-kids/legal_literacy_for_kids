Week 13: Writing a Foundational Document

The Governance Project — Phase 4: The Micro-Charter

This is the week.

You've spent 12 weeks building up the parts. Rules. Contracts. Letter vs. intent. Loopholes. Precedent. Rights. Distributed power. Now you put it all together into a single foundational document for a domain you actually care about.

It's called a Micro-Charter. Think of it as a constitution for something small but real — your bedroom, your friend group, a Minecraft server, a sibling alliance, a family meeting structure. Whatever you chose in Week 11, this is the week we write the foundational text.

By the end of this week, you'll have a document that includes: at least 3 explicit rules, at least 2 unalienable rights, a dispute-resolution protocol, clear definitions for ambiguous terms, and amendment procedures. It will be signed (or initialed, or stickered) by everyone in the domain. It will be real.

Facilitator Snapshot
  • This is the capstone artifact of Unit 4. Plan to spend the whole week on it.
  • The Charter must be for a real domain with real participants. A purely imaginary domain (a made-up country) is fine for the activity, but the Charter feels much more alive when it governs something the student actually participates in.
  • The student should not write the Charter in one sitting. Draft in Session 1, revise in Session 2, finalize in Independent Practice.
  • Save the final document. It will be the governing document for the mock trial in Week 17.

Prep time~15 minutes
MaterialsPaper (lots), pencil, the student's Case Notes for reference, the willing participants in the domain

DifficultyAdvanced

Facilitator Preparation​

Before You Begin
  • Confirm the student's Micro-Charter domain. It should be real and they should be able to actually have the other participants sign on.
  • Gather the student's Case Notes from Weeks 11 and 12 — those include candidate rights and power-distribution ideas.
  • Have a clean piece of paper or a fresh notebook page for the final document.
  • Make space for this week. The student should not be rushed.
Facilitation Mindset

This week, your job is structural editor. Don't write the Charter for the student. But when they write a vague rule, ask the questions you've been asking all curriculum: "What does that mean exactly?" "Who decides?" "What's the remedy?" The Charter should be airtight in spirit, even if it's still a kid's document in form.

For Younger Learners (Ages 8–9)​

Adapting This Week

Simplest version of the concept: "You're going to write the most important document of your life. It's the rules and rights for something you care about. Take your time."

What to shorten or skip:

  • Reduce to 2 rules, 1 right, and a simple "who decides if someone breaks a rule" protocol.
  • Skip amendment procedures — just leave a "this can be changed if we all agree" line.
  • Keep sessions to 20 minutes.

Adapting the activities:

  • The Charter can be illustrated. Drawings count fully.
  • Accept dictation. The facilitator can write what the student says.

Journal alternative: The Charter itself IS the journal entry this week. No additional writing needed.

What success looks like: The learner has a signed (or stickered) one-page document that says what the rules are and how disputes get handled.

For Ages 10–12
  • Push for a real preamble — a one-paragraph statement of the Charter's purpose.
  • Encourage the student to include a section on enumerated powers (what each role can and cannot do) in addition to rights.
  • Discuss real-world examples: the US Constitution's preamble, the UN Charter's opening, the Declaration of Independence's introduction. What do these documents share structurally?

Guided Session 1​

First Draft​

Learning Goal​

By the end of this session, the student can:

  • write a preamble stating the Charter's purpose
  • draft 3 explicit rules
  • draft 2 unalienable rights
  • sketch a dispute-resolution protocol

Activities​

1. The Preamble

Start with the why. The Charter needs a one-paragraph statement explaining what it's for.

Example:

"This Charter governs the Smith Family Game Nights. Its purpose is to make sure that every family member has a fair chance to play games they enjoy, that decisions about which games we play are made together, and that conflicts during play are handled in a way that everyone agrees is fair."

The preamble doesn't need to be fancy. It just needs to say:

  • What the Charter is for
  • Who it applies to
  • What it's trying to accomplish

Have the student write a preamble for their domain.

2. The Three Rules

Write at least three explicit rules. Each one should be:

  • Specific — not "be nice," but "no name-calling during play"
  • Enforceable — someone could actually verify whether it was followed
  • Justified — each rule should be paired with a reason (purpose clause from Week 9)

Example, for a friend group Charter:

Rule 1: Group decisions about meeting places are made by full-group agreement, not by whichever person speaks loudest. Purpose: prevent one person from dominating where we hang out.

Rule 2: If someone in the group is excluded from a hangout, the rest of the group must communicate the reason directly to them, not behind their back. Purpose: no whispering campaigns.

Rule 3: New members can join the group only by majority consent of existing members, who must explain their objections honestly if they have any. Purpose: keep group entry deliberate, not arbitrary.

3. The Two Unalienable Rights

Now the harder part. Unalienable rights are protections that even a majority vote cannot override. They are the firewalls.

For each right, write:

  • What the right is.
  • What it prevents the group from doing.
  • Why this protection matters specifically for this domain.

Example:

Right 1: The Right to Be Heard. No member can be banned, excluded, or punished without first being given a chance to respond to the accusation. Even if every other member agrees on the punishment, the accused must speak first. Why: silent banishment is the most common abuse in friend groups.

Right 2: The Right to Leave. No member can be forced to participate in any activity. Anyone may opt out of any specific hangout, game, or conversation without losing their place in the group. Why: peer pressure should never become structural.

The rights should be things that a majority might actually want to override. If everyone always agrees with the right, it's not doing any work.

4. The Dispute-Resolution Protocol

How do conflicts get resolved? This is where Week 12's distributed-power thinking comes in.

Write the protocol with these elements:

  • Who hears disputes? (One person? A panel? Rotating role?)
  • What's the process? (Each side speaks? Evidence?)
  • What's the remedy if someone is found to have violated a rule? (Warning, suspension, compensation?)
  • What if the dispute-resolver is biased or absent? (Backup mechanism.)

Example:

When a member alleges that a rule was broken, the following procedure applies:

  1. Both sides write down their account.
  2. A neutral third member (rotated each month) reads both accounts.
  3. Each side gets to respond once.
  4. The neutral third member makes a ruling within 24 hours.
  5. If the dispute involves the neutral third member, the role rotates to the next member on the list.
  6. The ruling can be appealed to a full-group vote, but only once.

Guided Session 2​

Revision and Refinement​

Learning Goal​

By the end of this session, the student can:

  • review their draft Charter for ambiguity, loopholes, and gaps
  • add definitions for unclear terms
  • write the amendment procedure
  • prepare the final document for signing

Activities​

1. The Loophole Audit

Bring the loophole-hunting skill from Week 9 back. For each rule and right in the Charter, ask:

  • Could a clever member follow the letter while violating the intent?
  • Is there a way to "comply" with the rule that we wouldn't want to allow?
  • If so, do we patch the rule, or strengthen the purpose clause?

Have the student amend the rules until each one has a defensible interpretation.

2. The Definitions Section

Every Charter needs a few definitions, because words mean different things to different people. Add a Definitions section that defines any term in the Charter that could be ambiguous.

Examples:

  • "Member" means any person who has signed this Charter.
  • "Meeting" means any planned gathering of 2 or more members, communicated at least 24 hours in advance.
  • "Hangout" means any social interaction, planned or unplanned, involving 2 or more members.
  • "Banned" means denied access to all future meetings and hangouts for the specified duration.

The Charter's quality depends on how precisely it defines its own terms.

3. The Amendment Procedure

Every foundational document needs a way to be changed — otherwise it becomes obsolete. Write the amendment procedure:

  • What's required to propose an amendment?
  • What's required to ratify (approve) one?
  • Are there parts of the Charter that are harder to amend than others? (Many real constitutions make rights harder to amend than ordinary rules.)
  • Is there a "sunset" — do amendments need to be re-ratified after some time?

Example:

Amendment Procedure

  • Any member may propose an amendment by writing it down and presenting it at a group meeting.
  • Amendments to rules require majority approval (more than half of members).
  • Amendments to rights require supermajority approval (at least 75% of members) — this is harder on purpose, because rights are supposed to be more permanent.
  • Once ratified, an amendment is added to the Charter with the date and the names of all who voted for it.

4. Final Layout and Signing

Lay out the Charter on a clean piece of paper or in a notebook. Standard structure:

═══════════════════════════════════════════════
THE [NAME] CHARTER
A Foundational Document
═══════════════════════════════════════════════

PREAMBLE
[One-paragraph statement of purpose]

DEFINITIONS
[Key terms and what they mean]

RULES
1. [Rule]
   Purpose: [Why this rule exists]
2. [Rule]
   Purpose: [...]
3. [Rule]
   Purpose: [...]

UNALIENABLE RIGHTS
1. [Right]
   What it prevents: [...]
2. [Right]
   What it prevents: [...]

DISPUTE-RESOLUTION PROTOCOL
[Step-by-step procedure for handling violations]

AMENDMENT PROCEDURE
[How this Charter can be changed]

SIGNATURES
[All members sign]
Date Ratified: ___________

The student presents this to the participants in the domain (siblings, friends, family members) and they all sign. If anyone refuses or wants changes, that's a negotiation — and the student gets to feel what it's like to ratify a real document.

This is a real artifact. Display it. Use it. Refer back to it when disputes happen.

Independent Practice​

Goal​

Live with the Charter for a few days and watch what happens.

Activities​

1. The First Days of the Charter

For the rest of the week, the Charter is the governing document of the domain. Whenever a dispute or question comes up:

  • Refer to the Charter.
  • Apply its rules, rights, and procedures.
  • Note any moments where the Charter worked well.
  • Note any moments where the Charter was incomplete or ambiguous.

These notes will become the input for Week 14 (stress testing).

2. The Soft Stress Test

Imagine one mildly tricky situation that might come up in your domain. Walk through how your Charter would handle it. Write:

  • The situation.
  • What the Charter says (or doesn't).
  • How you'd rule using the Charter.

(Week 14 will go much harder on this. This is just the warm-up.)

Case Notes​

This week, your Case Notes entry is the Charter itself. Tape a copy of the final signed Charter into your Case Notes, or write:

Date Ratified:

Signatures (all members):

One thing I'm proud of in this Charter:

One thing I'm worried might not work:

Reflection Questions​

  • Did writing the Charter change how the members talked about the domain? Did they take it more seriously?
  • Was there any part of the Charter that someone else wanted to change before they'd sign? What did you compromise on?
  • What did you leave out of the Charter that you wish you'd included?

Check for Understanding​

After this week, check whether the learner can:

  1. Show the document: "Can I see your Charter?" (Looking for: a real, signed Charter with all the required sections.)
  2. Explain a right: "Tell me about one of your unalienable rights. What does it protect, and why does it matter?" (Looking for: a real defense of why this thing needs majority-vote protection.)
  3. Walk through dispute resolution: "Imagine someone broke a rule. How would you find out and what would happen?" (Looking for: a clear procedure from accusation to ruling.)

If the learner can do at least 2 of these, they're ready for Week 14.

Pause and Notice​

What Matters Here

After the Charter is signed, ask:

"Did anything about getting other people to sign your Charter surprise you?"

Most students experience real friction in the ratification process. People want to negotiate. People want changes. People want to know what they're agreeing to.

This is exactly what happens with real foundational documents. The US Constitution was negotiated for months — and even then, ratification required ten amendments (the Bill of Rights) before some states would sign on.

"There's a moment in any group when someone signs a document and the relationships change. Before signing, you're just 'people who hang out together.' After signing, you're 'members of a system.' That shift can feel small but it's actually huge."

This week's takeaway: A Charter doesn't just describe the rules. It changes the kind of community you have. Once everyone has signed something, you've moved from informal trust to formal commitment. That's a real change — and it's why this exercise matters.

Spiral Review​

Connecting to Earlier Weeks

From every prior week: Look at your Charter. Can you find a piece of every unit in it?

  • Rules (Weeks 1–3): Your three rules.
  • Contracts (Weeks 4–7): The Charter itself is a multi-party contract.
  • Interpretation (Weeks 8–10): Your definitions and purpose clauses are anti-loophole and anti-ambiguity moves.
  • Rights and distributed power (Weeks 11–12): Your unalienable rights and dispute-resolution protocol.

If anything is missing, ask whether the Charter is complete.

Simplify (Ages 8–9)

Reduce the Charter to one page with four sections: (1) What this Charter is for. (2) Two rules. (3) One right that even a vote can't take away. (4) Who decides if someone breaks a rule. Skip amendment procedures and definitions sections.

Extend (Ages 10–12)

Have older learners study the structure of one real foundational document (the US Constitution, the UN Charter, or the foundational document of any country they're connected to). Identify the parallels: preamble, rules, rights, dispute-resolution, amendments. What does the student's Micro-Charter share with these documents? What does it lack?

Vocabulary This Week
  • Charter — A foundational document that establishes the rules and structure of a system.
  • Preamble — The opening paragraph stating the purpose of the document.
  • Unalienable — Cannot be taken away or transferred, even by majority vote.
  • Ratify — To formally approve and adopt a document.
  • Amendment — A formal change to a document, usually following a specified procedure.

Preview of Next Week​

Next week, we break it. Your beautiful Charter is about to face hypothetical conflicts designed to find every gap, ambiguity, and loophole. By the end of Week 14, you'll have proposed amendments and patched the document so it's actually durable. This is the stress-testing phase.
