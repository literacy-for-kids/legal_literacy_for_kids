Week 8: What the Rule Says vs. What It Means

Debugging the Code — Phase 3 Begins: No Vehicles in the Park

We are going to spend this entire week on one sentence.

The sentence is: "No vehicles in the park."

Posted on a sign at the entrance. Five words. Looks simple. And somehow, almost the entire field of legal interpretation can be illustrated by figuring out what this sentence actually means.

Does it ban:

  • A bicycle?
  • A wheelchair?
  • An ambulance responding to an emergency?
  • A baby stroller?
  • A skateboard?
  • A toy remote-control car?
  • A decommissioned tank used as a war memorial?

This is the famous problem that legal philosophers have been chewing on for almost a century. It opens up the central insight of Unit 3: rules are written in natural language, and natural language is ambiguous. Every interpretive question is really the question: what was this rule for?

Facilitator Snapshot
  • This week and next sit on a single thought experiment. The student should leave both weeks deeply familiar with "No vehicles in the park."
  • The point is not to get the "right" answer to each scenario. There often isn't one. The point is to understand the method of interpretation.
  • The two key concepts: letter (the literal words) and intent (what the rule was for). Most interpretation involves balancing these.
  • Resist giving "the answer." Encourage the student to argue both ways for the same scenario.

Week at a Glance​

Prep time~10 minutes
MaterialsPaper, pencil, the list of vehicle scenarios, Case Notes
Key vocabularyletter (of the law), intent, interpretation, ambiguity, plain meaning
DifficultyModerate to Advanced

Facilitator Preparation​

Before You Begin
  • Read all 8 scenarios in Session 1 before teaching so you can react naturally to the student's reasoning.
  • Have the student's Case Notes ready — they'll be capturing how they ruled and why.
  • You may want to read up briefly on the H.L.A. Hart vs. Lon Fuller "vehicles in the park" debate from the 1950s — it's where this thought experiment comes from, and the real legal scholarly disagreement is fascinating background.
Facilitation Mindset

The temptation is to lead the student to the "right" interpretation. Don't. The best legal minds disagree on these questions. Your job is to help the student notice that they're disagreeing with themselves as they think through it.

For Younger Learners (Ages 8–9)​

Adapting This Week

Simplest version of the concept: "Rules are written in words. Sometimes the words don't quite say what the person making the rule meant. We have to figure out what they really meant."

What to shorten or skip:

  • Use 4–5 scenarios instead of 8.
  • Skip the H.L.A. Hart background entirely.
  • Keep sessions to 20 minutes.

Adapting the activities:

  • Have the student draw each scenario before ruling on it. Visualizing helps.
  • Accept oral rulings ("I'd let the wheelchair in because...").

Journal alternative: Draw the park entrance. Draw four things going in or being turned away.

What success looks like: The learner can name a scenario where the literal rule says one thing but the rule's purpose says another.

For Ages 10–12
  • Introduce the classic distinction between plain meaning (what the words literally say) and purposive interpretation (what the rule was designed to accomplish).
  • Discuss the actual 1950s legal scholarship debate between H.L.A. Hart and Lon Fuller. They disagreed about whether "vehicle" has a clear "core meaning" with fuzzy edges, or whether even the core is contestable.
  • Have them write a brief opinion explaining their reasoning, like a mini judicial opinion.

Guided Session 1​

The Sign at the Park​

Learning Goal​

By the end of this session, the student can:

  • read a rule and notice where it could be interpreted multiple ways
  • propose at least two different readings for the same rule
  • begin to articulate what makes one interpretation more defensible than another

Activities​

1. Introduce the Sign

Set the scene:

"Imagine there's a beautiful public park in your town. At the entrance, there's a sign that reads:"

NO VEHICLES IN THE PARK.

"That's the whole rule. Five words. Now I'm going to bring you a series of cases. You are the judge. For each one, you have to decide: does this thing violate the rule? You'll tell me how you'd rule, and why."

The student now sits as judge.

2. The Cases

Walk through each one. Let the student rule. Push back on their reasoning. Encourage them to argue with themselves.

Case 1: A teenager rides a motorcycle into the park. (Almost everyone agrees: violates the rule. This is the "core" case.)

Case 2: A toddler is being pushed in a stroller. (Most people say: doesn't violate. But why not? It does have wheels and moves.)

Case 3: A child rides a bicycle through the park. (Most people say: violates. But some say it depends — what about a bike path? Is a bike a "vehicle" the same way a car is?)

Case 4: A person in a wheelchair rolls through the park. (Almost everyone agrees: doesn't violate. But why? It has wheels and carries a person. The answer usually involves the wheelchair's purpose — mobility, not transportation or recreation.)

Case 5: An ambulance drives through the park to reach an injured person. (Almost everyone agrees: doesn't violate, or shouldn't. But strictly — the sign says no vehicles. Period. The ambulance is a vehicle.)

Case 6: A delivery truck drives in to deliver supplies to the park's snack bar. (This one splits opinions. The truck is clearly a vehicle. It's there for the park's own operations. Does that matter?)

Case 7: A child rides a Segway / hoverboard / electric scooter. (Splits opinions. Newer technology that the sign-makers couldn't have anticipated. Are these vehicles?)

Case 8: The town wants to put a decommissioned military tank in the park as a war memorial. It cannot move — its engine has been removed. (This is the famous case from H.L.A. Hart's 1958 article. The tank is obviously a "vehicle" in any normal sense. But it's a monument, not transportation. Should the rule apply?)

3. Notice the Pattern

After all 8 cases, ask:

"Did you ever feel like the rule was clearly saying one thing, but you wanted to rule the other way?"

This is the central tension of legal interpretation. The student has just experienced it.

"When that happened, what were you doing? What were you thinking about?"

Most students realize: they were trying to figure out what the rule was for. Was it about safety? Noise? Pollution? Keeping the park peaceful? Each of those purposes leads to different answers in different cases.

Drop the terminology:

"When we follow the exact words of a rule, we're using the letter of the law. When we follow the rule's purpose — what it was meant to do — we're using the intent of the law. Both matter. Sometimes they point in the same direction. Sometimes they point in opposite directions. That's the hard part."

Guided Session 2​

Letter vs. Intent​

Learning Goal​

By the end of this session, the student can:

  • explicitly distinguish between letter and intent
  • argue both sides of an interpretive question
  • recognize that real judges face this tension constantly

Activities​

1. The Two-Column Analysis

For three of the cases from Session 1, have the student fill out this table:

CaseWhat the LETTER saysWhat the INTENT seems to sayMy rulingWhy

Example:

CaseWhat the LETTER saysWhat the INTENT seems to sayMy rulingWhy
Ambulance"No vehicles" — ambulance is a vehicle. Violates.The rule is about keeping the park peaceful and safe. An ambulance saves lives. Letting it through serves safety, which is the rule's purpose.Allow it.Intent wins over letter when life is at stake.

The student writes their reasoning. There's no single right answer. The reasoning is what matters.

2. The Switch-Sides Drill

Pick one case (the tank memorial is great for this). Have the student argue:

"First, argue that the tank should be banned from the park. Use only the letter of the rule."

Let them argue. Then:

"Now, argue that the tank should be allowed. Use only the intent of the rule."

Let them argue. Then:

"Now — which argument is stronger? Or do you think reasonable people could disagree?"

The point is to demonstrate that good lawyers can argue either side of most interpretive cases. The student should feel both sides have real force.

3. The Sign-Maker's Question

Now zoom out:

"Imagine you were the person who designed this sign. You only had five words. What did you actually want to prevent?"

Common answers:

  • Loud, polluting cars driving through and ruining the peace
  • Speed and danger to pedestrians
  • Wear and tear on the grass and paths
  • General "this is a place for people, not machines"

Now ask:

"Knowing what you really wanted to prevent — how would you re-write the sign so it's less ambiguous?"

Some attempts:

  • "NO MOTORIZED VEHICLES IN THE PARK." (Catches cars but allows bikes.)
  • "NO VEHICLES OVER 50 LBS." (Weight-based.)
  • "NO VEHICLES, EXCEPT EMERGENCY AND MAINTENANCE." (Carve-outs.)
  • "PEDESTRIANS, BIKES, AND WHEELCHAIRS ONLY." (Whitelist instead of blacklist.)

Notice: even the rewrites have ambiguities. What's an "emergency"? What's "motorized"? An e-bike? You can never fully escape ambiguity. You can only choose which ambiguities you'd rather have.

Independent Practice​

Goal​

Notice ambiguous rules in the student's actual life and try to interpret them with both letter and intent in mind.

Activities​

1. The Ambiguous Rule Hunt

The student finds three rules in their actual life — at home, at school, in their neighborhood — that have some ambiguity. For each, write:

  • The rule, exactly as worded.
  • One case where the rule is clearly violated.
  • One case where it's not clear if the rule applies.
  • What you think the rule's intent is.
  • How you'd rule on the unclear case.

Examples:

  • "No screens at the dinner table." (Clear case: phone. Unclear case: a smartwatch glowing. Intent: family connection. Ruling: probably not allowed, even though it's tiny.)
  • "Be home before dark." (Clear case: being out at 10 PM in winter. Unclear case: walking up the driveway at the exact moment the sun sets. Intent: safety. Ruling: close enough is close enough.)

2. The Sign Designer Challenge

Pick one of the ambiguous rules from your life and try to re-write it so it's clearer. Notice what new ambiguities your re-write creates.

Case Notes​

Add this to your Case Notes:

  • My ruling:
  • Why I ruled that way:

An ambiguous rule in my real life:

  • The letter:
  • The intent:
  • A case where they pull in opposite directions:

My attempted re-write of the rule — and the new ambiguity it created:

Sentence starters for younger learners:

  • "The rule says ___."
  • "But what it really means is ___."
  • "A tricky example is ___."

Reflection Questions​

  • Have you ever followed the letter of a rule while clearly violating its intent? (Like staying technically within bedtime but watching TV in the dark with headphones?)
  • Why don't rule-makers just write down every possible situation?
  • If you were a judge, would you rather follow the letter or the intent when they disagree? Why?

Check for Understanding​

After this week, check whether the learner can:

  1. Define the distinction: "What's the difference between the letter and the intent of a rule?" (Looking for: letter = exact words, intent = what the rule was for.)
  2. Argue both ways: "Should the tank be allowed in the park? Argue the case both ways." (Looking for: real reasoning on both sides.)
  3. Spot ambiguity: "Tell me a real rule from your life that's ambiguous." (Looking for: a real example with explanation of where the ambiguity lives.)

If the learner can do at least 2 of these, they're ready for Week 9.

Pause and Notice​

What Matters Here

After working through several scenarios, ask:

"When you imagine being on the other side of a rule — the person who would be turned away, or punished, or told no — does the letter or the intent feel more fair?"

The answer depends on which side of the rule you're on. For the rule-maker, the letter is comforting — it's clear and predictable. For the person being judged by the rule, the intent often feels more fair — because the intent is about what you actually did, not just what you literally did.

This is a real tension that runs through every legal system. Different countries and different judges lean different ways. Neither approach is "right." Both have strengths and weaknesses.

This week's takeaway: Rules are written in words, but words can never capture every situation in advance. The job of interpretation is figuring out which reading is closest to what the rule was for — and reasonable people can disagree about the answer.

Spiral Review​

Connecting to Earlier Weeks

From Week 7: When you wrote your Chores API, you ran into this exact problem. "Cleaning the kitchen" — what does that actually include? The work of specifying inputs and edge cases is interpretation in advance.

From Week 3: Your Three Laws for the island had to be interpreted too. Look back at them now — which ones have ambiguity you didn't notice at the time?

Simplify (Ages 8–9)

Use only 4 scenarios from the list (motorcycle, stroller, bicycle, ambulance). Skip the tank and the Segway. Focus on the basic idea: "the rule says one thing but probably means another."

Extend (Ages 10–12)

Introduce H.L.A. Hart's idea (from 1958) that every rule has a "core" of clear cases and a "penumbra" of fuzzy ones. The motorcycle is clearly in the core. The tank is clearly in the penumbra. Discuss whether the core itself can be questioned, or whether it's the stable part.

Vocabulary This Week
  • Letter (of the law) — The literal, word-for-word reading of a rule.
  • Intent — The purpose the rule was designed to serve.
  • Interpretation — The process of figuring out how a rule applies to a specific situation.
  • Ambiguity — When a rule could reasonably be read more than one way.
  • Plain meaning — The most obvious, ordinary reading of a rule's words.
