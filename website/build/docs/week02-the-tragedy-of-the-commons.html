Week 2: What Happens Without Shared Rules

The Architecture of Rules — Phase 1

Last week we asked the big question: why do rules exist at all?

This week we zoom in on a specific kind of problem that almost always requires rules to solve: shared resources. A pond everyone fishes from. A pasture everyone grazes their animals on. A bathroom everyone in the house uses. A WiFi router every device in the house competes for.

These are called commons — and they have a built-in bug. Each individual person, acting completely rationally, has a tiny incentive to take a little more than their fair share. And when every individual makes that perfectly reasonable choice, the shared resource collapses. This is called the tragedy of the commons, and it is one of the most important patterns in any legal system.

Facilitator Snapshot
  • The key insight this week: a system can crash even when no single person is being malicious. Everyone can be acting "reasonably" and the result is still disaster.
  • The classic fishing game (described below) does most of the teaching. Don't skip it.
  • This week sets up Week 3, where students draft their first laws to solve a commons problem.
  • Don't moralize. The point is not "people are selfish." The point is "the math of shared resources is hard, and that's why we need rules."

Week at a Glance​

Prep time~15 minutes
MaterialsA bowl of small objects (about 30 — pennies, beans, candies, or paperclips), paper, pencil, Case Notes
Key vocabularycommons, free rider, sustainable, collapse, regulation
DifficultyIntroductory

Facilitator Preparation​

Before You Begin
  • Set up the fishing game ahead of time: a bowl of ~30 small countable objects.
  • Have a way to track rounds: paper and pencil is fine.
  • Read through the rules of the fishing game once so you can run it without checking notes mid-game.
  • Be ready to play one or two rounds. With multiple students, the game is richer.
Facilitation Mindset

Resist the urge to warn the student during the fishing game. The collapse is the lesson. If the student takes 10 fish on round 1 and the pond dies, that's perfect — let it happen, then ask why.

For Younger Learners (Ages 8–9)​

Adapting This Week

Simplest version of the concept: "When something belongs to everyone, it's really easy for everyone to use it up without meaning to."

What to shorten or skip:

  • Skip the "free rider" vocabulary word. Just talk about "the person who lets everyone else do the work."
  • Play one round of the fishing game, not three.
  • Keep sessions to 20 minutes.

Adapting the activities:

  • Use bigger, more obvious commons examples (a candy bowl, a pile of toys to share).
  • Draw the pond collapse instead of writing about it.

Journal alternative: Draw the pond before and after. Label one "shared and cared for" and one "everyone took too much."

What success looks like: The learner can name something they share with others and explain one rule that protects it.

For Ages 10–12
  • After the game, calculate explicitly: how many fish were taken total? How many would the pond have produced if everyone had taken less?
  • Introduce sustainability as a vocabulary word.
  • Discuss whether the problem changes if the players can talk to each other (it does — coordination is half the solution).

Guided Session 1​

The Fishing Game​

Learning Goal​

By the end of this session, the student can:

  • describe what a commons is and give an example
  • explain how individually rational decisions can produce a collectively terrible outcome
  • recognize the pattern of commons collapse in real-world systems

Activities​

1. Set Up the Game

Put a bowl of ~30 objects in the middle of the table. Tell the student:

"This bowl is a pond. The objects are fish. Every round, you can take as many fish as you want. At the end of each round, however many fish are left in the pond will double, because they reproduce. Goal: collect as many fish as you can over 5 rounds without killing the pond. If the pond ever hits zero, the game is over — no more fish forever."

If you're playing with multiple students, every player takes from the same pond.

If you're playing one-on-one, you (the facilitator) play too, and you play honestly, meaning you take a moderate number of fish, demonstrating sustainable behavior. Don't tell the student what to do.

2. Play the Game

Round 1: Each player takes fish. Then count what's left, and double it (up to the bowl's capacity, ~30). Round 2: Same. Continue until either:

  • The pond collapses (hits zero), or
  • You finish 5 rounds with the pond still alive.

In most one-on-one games, this works as follows:

  • If the student takes many fish on round 1, the pond can't recover and dies quickly.
  • If the student takes few, the pond stays healthy and they end up with more total fish than if they had been greedy.

3. Debrief

After the game, ask:

"Did the pond survive? Why or why not?"

"If you played again, what would you do differently?"

Then drop the big idea:

"What you just experienced is called the tragedy of the commons. When something belongs to everyone — a fishing pond, a forest, a public park, the WiFi at your house — every individual person has a tiny incentive to take a little extra. And when everyone takes a little extra, the resource dies. The math is brutal: nobody has to be a villain for the system to crash."

Optional: if the pond died quickly, replay the game with a rule: "No more than 3 fish per round per player." Watch how the rule changes the outcome.

Guided Session 2​

Commons Everywhere​

Learning Goal​

By the end of this session, the student can:

  • identify commons in their daily life
  • predict which commons will collapse and which will hold up
  • name a rule that protects a real-world commons

Activities​

1. The Commons Hunt

Together, list all the commons in the student's life. A commons is anything where the resource is shared and no one person owns it.

Possible CommonsWho Shares ItWhat Could Go Wrong
Family WiFiEveryone at homeSomeone hogs all the bandwidth
Snack drawerFamily or roommatesLast person eats the last snack
Public parkThe whole neighborhoodTrash, damage, overcrowding
Class suppliesEveryone in the classSomeone takes all the good markers
Group chatAll membersOne person spams it
The oceanEveryone on EarthOverfishing, pollution
Earth's atmosphereEveryone on EarthPollution, climate effects

Aim for at least 6 examples.

2. The Free Rider

Introduce the free rider problem:

"A free rider is someone who benefits from a system without contributing to it. Imagine four people share a fridge and everyone is supposed to take turns cleaning it. If one person never cleans, but eats from the fridge anyway — they're a free rider."

Ask:

"Have you ever been a free rider? Have you ever been the person doing the cleaning while a free rider got the benefit?"

Most students have stories. Let them share. The point: free riding is not unusual or evil — it's a structural temptation that exists everywhere there's a commons.

3. Spot the Rule

For each commons on the list, ask:

"Is there a rule that protects this commons? Who made it?"

Examples:

  • Public park → "Don't litter," "No glass bottles," park hours
  • Family WiFi → "No streaming during dinner," "Homework before YouTube"
  • Class markers → "Only take what you need," "Put them back"
  • Ocean → Fishing quotas (this is a real law)
  • Atmosphere → Pollution regulations (also real laws)

Notice: every commons needs some kind of rule, or it dies. The rule might be a law, a household agreement, or a custom — but something has to manage the shared use, or the math wins.

Independent Practice​

Goal​

Find a commons in the student's own life and design a rule to protect it.

Activities​

1. Identify a Personal Commons

The student picks one specific commons from their actual life. Examples:

  • The family snack drawer
  • The shared bathroom
  • Their bedroom (if shared with a sibling)
  • The TV remote
  • The shared family computer
  • The family car
  • A friend group's group chat

Write down:

  • What the commons is
  • Who shares it
  • What kind of "collapse" looks like for this commons (e.g., the snack drawer always being empty, the bathroom always being a mess, the group chat being unreadable from spam)

2. Design a Rule

The student writes one rule that would protect this commons. The rule must:

  • Be specific (not "be nice")
  • Be enforceable (someone could actually check whether it was followed)
  • Apply equally to everyone

Examples:

  • "Whoever takes the last of a snack writes it on the grocery list."
  • "After using the shared bathroom, wipe down the counter."
  • "In the group chat, no more than 5 messages in a row without giving others a chance to reply."

Bonus: write what the consequence is if someone breaks the rule. (Not a punishment — just what happens next. "If you forget to add to the grocery list, you're responsible for going to get it.")

Sentence starters for younger learners:

  • "Something I share with ___ is ___."
  • "If we all took too much, ___ would happen."
  • "My rule to protect it is ___."

Reflection Questions​

  • Did anyone try to free ride in your house this week? How did it feel to notice?
  • Is there a commons in your life that you think is working well? What's the rule (written or unwritten) that's keeping it healthy?
  • If the commons is in trouble, do you think you could propose your rule to the people who share it?

Check for Understanding​

After this week, check whether the learner can:

  1. Define a commons in their own words: "What's a commons?" (Looking for: something shared, no one owns it, everyone uses it.)
  2. Explain the tragedy in one sentence: "Why does a commons sometimes collapse?" (Looking for: when everyone takes a little extra, it adds up to too much.)
  3. Name a real-world example: "Tell me one commons in your life and a rule that protects it." (Looking for: any specific example with a real rule.)

If the learner can do at least 2 of these, they're ready for Week 3.

Pause and Notice​

What Matters Here

After the fishing game, ask:

"How did it feel when the pond ran out — or got close? Was there a moment when you wanted to grab more fish before someone else did?"

This is the real lesson of the week. The tragedy of the commons is not really about ponds. It's about a feeling — the moment you realize that being patient might hurt you if no one else is. Once that feeling kicks in, the math of the commons starts running by itself.

"Now imagine everyone in the world feels that same way at the same time. That's why so many of the most important laws in the world exist — to protect shared things from a perfectly natural, perfectly human feeling."

This week's takeaway: When something belongs to everyone, it belongs to no one — unless we agree, in advance, on rules that keep it alive.

Spiral Review​

Connecting to Earlier Weeks

From Week 1: We learned that rules exist for reasons — usually safety, fairness, or coordination. This week we discovered a fourth reason: protecting shared things from being accidentally destroyed. Most environmental laws, traffic laws, and public-space laws are commons-protection rules.

Simplify (Ages 8–9)

Use a candy jar instead of an abstract pond. Refill it visibly between rounds. The visual reinforces the doubling. Skip the "free rider" term.

Extend (Ages 10–12)

Discuss whether being able to talk to each other before each round changes the outcome of the fishing game (it does — coordination beats independence). Ask: "Why is talking allowed in some commons situations and impossible in others?" (Hint: think about why pollution is so hard to solve.)

Vocabulary This Week
  • Commons — A resource that belongs to everyone and can be used by anyone.
  • Tragedy of the commons — When individuals acting in self-interest overuse a shared resource and destroy it for everyone.
  • Free rider — Someone who benefits from a shared system without contributing to it.
  • Sustainable — Something that can keep going without running out. The opposite of "collapsing."
  • Regulation — A rule that manages how a shared resource is used, to keep it sustainable.
