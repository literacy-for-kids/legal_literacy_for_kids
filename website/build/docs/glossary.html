Glossary

This glossary collects the key terms used throughout the Legal Literacy for Kids curriculum. Each entry includes a short, kid-friendly definition and the week where the term was first introduced. If a term feels fuzzy, re-read it here, then go back to the week where it lives — the term will land more solidly with the surrounding context.

A reminder: this is an educational systems toolkit, not legal advice. The definitions here are designed to teach clear thinking about rules and systems, not to be cited in a real legal matter.

Terms​

TermDefinitionFirst Introduced
AcceptanceA clear agreement to the exact terms of an offer, without changes. The "handshake" half of a contract.Week 4
AmbiguityWhen a rule could reasonably be read more than one way. Most legal interpretation is the work of resolving ambiguity.Week 8
AmendmentA formal change to a document, usually following a specified procedure.Week 13
Anti-circumventionRules specifically designed to stop people from gaming other rules. A defense against loopholes.Week 9
AppealA request to a higher court to review a ruling because the process was flawed — not just because you lost.Week 18
Beyond reasonable doubtThe very high standard of proof used in criminal cases. Closer to 99% certain than to 51% certain.Week 15
BindingRequired to be followed; not just a polite intention. Contracts and precedents can both be binding.Week 4
BreachFailing to follow through on a contractual obligation.Week 7
Burden of proofThe job of proving something in a dispute, and the standard of certainty required to win.Week 15
Case NotesThe running journal kept through all 18 weeks, where the student records rules they noticed and rulings they made.Intro
CharterA foundational document that establishes the rules and structure of a system.Week 13
Checks and balancesA design where each part of a system can correct mistakes in the other parts.Week 12
Civil lawThe branch of law where one party sues another over a private harm. Standard of proof: preponderance of evidence.Optional Week 1
CommonsA resource that belongs to everyone and can be used by anyone.Week 2
ConsiderationThe value each side gives in a contract. Can be a thing, an action, a promise, or even silence.Week 4
ContractA binding agreement between two or more parties, made up of an offer, an acceptance, and consideration.Week 4
Counter-offerChanging any term of the offer. This kills the original offer and creates a new one.Week 5
Criminal lawThe branch of law where the state prosecutes someone on behalf of society. Standard of proof: beyond reasonable doubt.Optional Week 1
CustomAn unwritten rule that everyone follows anyway, like taking shoes off at the door.Week 1
DistinguishTo argue that a new case is meaningfully different from an old precedent, so the precedent doesn't apply.Week 10
Distributed powerAuthority spread across multiple sources so no single one can override the system.Week 12
Due processThe structural guarantee that the right process must be followed before any legal consequence — even when the answer seems obvious.Week 16
Edge caseAn unusual situation the basic rule doesn't quite handle.Week 7
EnforcementHow a rule is actually applied. Who checks? What happens if it's broken?Week 3
FirewallA hardcoded barrier in a system. Rights act as firewalls between institutions and individuals.Week 11
ForbearanceAgreeing to not do something you had the right to do. Can count as consideration in a contract.Week 6
Foundational lawA law written at the start of a system, often harder to change than later laws.Week 3
Free riderSomeone who benefits from a shared system without contributing to it.Week 2
GiftA one-way transfer of value with no consideration in return. Different from a contract.Week 4
IntentThe purpose the rule was designed to serve, as opposed to its exact words.Week 8
InterpretationThe process of figuring out how a rule applies to a specific situation.Week 8
Invitation to treatA display (like a price tag or menu) that looks like an offer but is actually inviting the customer to make the offer.Week 5
JurisdictionWhich set of rules applies, and which court has authority to rule on a case.Week 3
LawA rule written down by a government, with official consequences for breaking it.Week 1
Letter (of the law)The literal, word-for-word reading of a rule.Week 8
LoopholeA way to follow the letter of a rule while violating its intent.Week 9
MajorityThe larger side in a group vote. Majorities usually win, but rights block them on protected issues.Week 11
Meeting of the mindsWhen both sides actually agree on the same exact terms of a contract.Week 5
Micro-CharterThe foundational document the student writes in Week 13 — a constitution for a real domain they care about.Week 13
Mutual obligationBoth sides of a contract are committed to do something. The defining feature of a contract.Week 6
OfferA clear, specific statement of what one party will do in exchange for what.Week 4
OverturnTo replace an old precedent with a new ruling. A bigger move than distinguishing.Week 10
PatchAn update to a rule that closes a specific loophole.Week 9
Plain meaningThe most obvious, ordinary reading of a rule's words.Week 8
PreambleThe opening paragraph of a foundational document, stating its purpose.Week 13
PrecedentA past ruling that becomes binding for similar future cases. The version-control system of the legal world.Week 10
PredictabilityKnowing roughly what's going to happen next. The thing rules create so that societies can scale.Week 1
Preponderance of evidenceThe standard of proof used in civil cases. Closer to 51% certain — "more likely than not."Week 15
Procedural due processThe right process was followed (notice, hearing, evidence, response).Week 16
Purpose clauseA statement in a rule explaining what the rule is intended to accomplish. A defense against loopholes.Week 9
RatifyTo formally approve and adopt a document.Week 13
RegulationA rule that manages how a shared resource is used, to keep it sustainable.Week 2
RemedyThe agreed-upon consequence when a breach happens — what the wronged party gets.Week 7
RightA constraint on institutional power that protects an individual, even from majority decisions.Week 11
RuleAn instruction that tells people what to do or not do.Week 1
Rule of lawThe principle that the rules apply equally and predictably to everyone, regardless of who they are.Intro
Separation of powersSeparating rule-making, enforcement, and interpretation into different bodies.Week 12
Single point of failureA component whose failure causes the whole system to fail.Week 12
SovereigntyThe principle that each nation's internal law is its own business — a firewall against outside jurisdiction.Optional Week 2
SpecificationA precise description of what is being done or provided. The more specific, the fewer disputes.Week 7
Spirit of the lawThe general intent and purpose of a rule, as opposed to its exact words.Week 9
StandingThe right to bring a case — usually because you were personally harmed by what happened.Week 15
Stare decisisLatin for "to stand by decided things." The principle that courts follow precedent.Week 10
Substantive due processThe outcome itself was fair — not just the process used to reach it.Week 16
SustainableSomething that can keep going without running out. The opposite of "collapsing."Week 2
SystemA collection of parts that work together. The legal system is the collection of laws, courts, and procedures.Week 1
Tragedy of the commonsWhen individuals acting in self-interest overuse a shared resource and destroy it for everyone.Week 2
TreatyA contract between nations: offer, acceptance, and consideration all apply, but enforcement is much harder.Optional Week 2
UnalienableCannot be taken away or transferred, even by majority vote. Often used to describe foundational rights.Week 11
VerdictThe formal judgment in a case — who wins, what remedy applies, and the reasoning behind it.Week 18

A note on legal terms​

These definitions are written for clarity, not for courtrooms. A real lawyer or judge might draw finer distinctions than this glossary does — especially around terms like standing, burden of proof, jurisdiction, and the line between civil and criminal. If you ever have a real legal question, talk to a real lawyer.

The point of this glossary is not to memorize. It is to give you a shared vocabulary so that you can read a rule, talk about a system, and notice when something is off. That is the foundation. Everything else builds on top of it.
