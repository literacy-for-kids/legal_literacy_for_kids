Week 17: Running a Full Proceeding

The Justice System in Action — Phase 5, Week 3

This is the application week.

Everything you've built — the Charter, the amendments, the Listening Protocol, the rules, the rights, the dispute-resolution court — gets put to work. A real dispute, adapted from your life, comes before your Charter court. You file a complaint. You gather evidence. You make arguments. You hear a ruling.

Before we start, the most important rule of this week:

This is not about winning. It is about running the system correctly.

If the student is the plaintiff and the court rules against them, the week is still a success — because the system worked. A well-run trial where the student loses their case is a far more valuable lesson than a poorly-run one where they win. Real lawyers learn this on Day 1. Run the procedure, accept the ruling, propose amendments later.

Facilitator Snapshot
  • This is the longest, most active week of the curriculum. Plan extra time and consider doing both sessions on the same day if scheduling permits.
  • Pick a real dispute. Not a hypothetical. A real one — adapted, anonymized, scaled down — but something with actual stakes for the student.
  • The governing document is the Micro-Charter. If the Charter is silent on something, the court must note the gap and rule from intent (Week 8 returns).
  • The facilitator is the judge. Resist the urge to coach the plaintiff. Treat the proceeding like a real one.
  • Solo and small-group fallbacks are below. A solo student can do this with the facilitator playing multiple roles. Don't skip the week.

Week at a Glance​

Prep time~30 minutes
MaterialsThe final Micro-Charter (with all amendments and the Listening Protocol), Case Notes, paper, pencil, any "evidence" the student wants to use, a designated trial space
Key vocabularyplaintiff, defendant, complaint, witness, evidence, deliberation, ruling
DifficultyCapstone

Facilitator Preparation​

Before You Begin
  • Pick the dispute together. It must be real, but small. Adapt it as needed. Examples: a borrowed item that was lost or damaged, a shared resource (TV time, snack supply) that was used unfairly, a Charter rule that the student believes another member violated. Avoid disputes involving real harm or strong emotions — this is a learning trial, not a therapy session.
  • Confirm the other party will play along. If a sibling is the defendant, they need to be a willing participant. If no one is available, the facilitator plays the defendant (see Solo Fallback).
  • Re-read the Charter and Listening Protocol before Session 2. The trial must actually follow the protocol the student wrote.
  • Designate a trial space. A kitchen table works fine. The point is having a physical setting that says "this is the proceeding."
  • Print or write the procedure (complaint → response → evidence → arguments → ruling) so it's visible during the trial. This is the procedural backbone.
Facilitation Mindset

You are the judge this week. Be formal. Be slow. Be procedural. Resist the urge to soften things if the student's case is weak — let them feel what it's like to argue a case that maybe isn't airtight, and let them experience either a real win or a real loss. That experience is the lesson.

Solo / Small-Group Fallback

If the student is solo (no sibling, no co-learner available to play defendant):

  • The facilitator plays both the judge and the defendant. To do this cleanly, designate a chair or a place for each role and physically move between them. ("Now I'm the defendant — and the defendant says...") Then move back to the judge chair to deliberate.
  • Alternative: a willing parent or older sibling plays the defendant; facilitator stays as judge.
  • Alternative: a stuffed animal or a written "statement of defense" prepared in advance can serve as the defendant. The student presents the case as if to a court that has the defendant's written response.

If there are 2–3 students:

  • One can be plaintiff, one defendant, one a witness or backup judge.
  • Rotate roles across different mock trials if you have time.

The structural lesson — procedure, evidence, ruling — works in all of these setups. Don't skip the week for lack of a perfect cast.

For Younger Learners (Ages 8–9)​

Adapting This Week

Simplest version of the concept: "We are going to run a trial — a real one, with rules, in your court — about something that actually happened. You'll be the lawyer. The grown-up will be the judge."

What to shorten or skip:

  • Skip opening and closing statements; just go straight to the facts and the arguments.
  • Reduce evidence to one or two items.
  • Use spoken arguments rather than written briefs.
  • Keep sessions to 25 minutes.

Adapting the activities:

  • Pick a very small, very recent dispute ("you took my marker and didn't give it back" works).
  • Use drawn evidence, props, or witness statements written by the facilitator.
  • Use simple words: "plaintiff" = "the one bringing the case." "Defendant" = "the one being accused." "Verdict" = "the answer."

Journal alternative: A drawing of the courtroom with each role labeled (judge, plaintiff, defendant) and one sentence saying what happened.

What success looks like: The learner ran a procedure from complaint to ruling, with both sides getting to speak.

For Ages 10–12
  • Push for written opening and closing statements, even short ones.
  • Require the plaintiff to cite a specific Charter provision in the complaint.
  • Have the student prepare a written case brief in advance — facts, rule, argument, remedy sought.
  • After the trial, the older student can write the judge's reasoning themselves as if they had to issue an opinion.

Guided Session 1​

Case Preparation​

Learning Goal​

By the end of this session, the student can:

  • file a formal complaint citing a specific Charter provision
  • gather evidence relevant to the case
  • prepare arguments connecting the facts to the rule

Activities​

1. Filing the Complaint

The complaint is the document that officially starts the case. It has four required parts:

PartWhat it says
The PartiesWho is the plaintiff (bringing the case) and who is the defendant (accused).
The FactsWhat happened, in plain language. No arguments yet — just what occurred.
The ViolationWhich Charter provision was violated, quoted from the Charter.
The Remedy SoughtWhat the plaintiff is asking the court to do (an apology, a return of property, a rule clarification, a temporary suspension of a member's privilege, etc.).

Have the student write a one-page complaint following that structure. Example:

═══════════════════════════════════════════════
COMPLAINT
The [Name] Charter Court
═══════════════════════════════════════════════

PLAINTIFF: [Student's name]
DEFENDANT: [Other party's name]
DATE OF FILING: ___________

FACTS:
On [date], the plaintiff and defendant were [context].
The defendant [what they did]. The plaintiff [what
happened as a result].

CHARTER PROVISION VIOLATED:
Rule [X] of the [Name] Charter, which states:
"[Quote the actual text of the rule]."

REMEDY SOUGHT:
The plaintiff respectfully requests that the court:
(a) [primary remedy]
(b) [secondary remedy if applicable]

Signed,
[Plaintiff]

The complaint is then formally given to the defendant — that's notice, the first step of the Listening Protocol from Week 16. The defendant now has time to prepare.

2. Gathering Evidence

Together with the student, list everything that could count as evidence:

  • Witnesses. Anyone who saw what happened. (For most household disputes: zero. That's fine.)
  • Physical evidence. A broken object. A written message. A photo.
  • Documentary evidence. Anything written that's relevant — a text message, a note, a journal entry.
  • The Charter itself. The provision being applied is itself evidence of what the system requires.

For each piece of evidence, the student writes:

  • What it is.
  • How it relates to the case.
  • What it proves (or helps prove).

Some pieces will be strong. Some will be weak. Some will turn out not to help at all. That's normal.

3. Preparing the Argument

The argument is the bridge between the facts and the ruling the plaintiff wants. It answers:

  • What rule applies?
  • How does the rule apply to these facts?
  • Why is the remedy I'm asking for the right one?

A good argument follows the IRAC structure (which lawyers actually use, though the student doesn't need the acronym):

  1. Issue: What question is the court being asked to answer?
  2. Rule: What does the Charter say?
  3. Application: How does the rule apply to what happened?
  4. Conclusion: What should the court do about it?

Have the student practice their argument out loud. They should be able to walk through all four steps without notes by the end of the session.

4. Anticipating the Defense

Before the trial, the plaintiff should think about the defendant's likely arguments. Common defenses:

  • "I didn't do what I'm accused of."
  • "I did it, but it wasn't a violation."
  • "I did it, but the rule shouldn't apply because [context]."
  • "I did it, but the Charter is unclear here."
  • "Even if I did it, the remedy sought is too harsh."

For each likely defense, the plaintiff prepares a brief response. This is exactly what real lawyers do before trial.

Guided Session 2​

The Trial​

Learning Goal​

By the end of this session, the student can:

  • participate in a structured proceeding from opening to ruling
  • present arguments and respond to opposing arguments
  • hear and accept a ruling, regardless of outcome

Activities​

1. Convene the Court

Open formally. The facilitator (judge) reads:

"The [Name] Charter Court is now in session. The case before us is [Plaintiff's name] v. [Defendant's name], regarding [brief description]. The governing document is the [Name] Charter and its amendments. The procedure for this hearing is the Listening Protocol of the Charter."

"Both parties have been given notice. Both parties have had time to prepare. Both parties have access to the evidence. We will now proceed."

This formality matters. It signals the shift from "kid argument" to "structured proceeding."

2. Opening Statements

Each side has a short opening statement (1–2 minutes max). The plaintiff goes first:

"Plaintiff, you have the floor. Briefly tell the court what you will be showing and what you are asking us to do."

Then the defendant:

"Defendant, you have the floor. Briefly tell the court what you will be arguing in your defense."

Both sides should stick to summary at this stage. No detailed evidence yet.

3. The Plaintiff's Case

The plaintiff presents their case in full:

  • The facts of what happened.
  • The Charter provision they say was violated.
  • Each piece of evidence, one at a time, with an explanation of what it shows.
  • Witnesses (if any) make their statements.

The defendant may not interrupt. They may take notes.

When the plaintiff is finished:

"The defendant may now respond."

4. The Defendant's Case

The defendant presents:

  • Their version of what happened.
  • Their interpretation of the Charter provision.
  • Any evidence they have.
  • Any witnesses.

The plaintiff may not interrupt.

5. The Judge's Questions

The facilitator-judge now gets to ask questions of either side:

  • "Plaintiff, you cited Rule 2 — but Rule 2 says [X]. Does what happened actually fall under Rule 2?"
  • "Defendant, you said the Charter is unclear here. Where exactly is the ambiguity?"
  • "Both parties — what does the purpose clause of this rule suggest about how it should apply here?"

This is the interpretation moment. Week 8 returns: if the Charter is ambiguous, what was the rule for? The judge probes both sides on how their reading fits the Charter's intent.

6. Closing Arguments

Each side gives a closing statement (1–2 minutes). Plaintiff first, defendant second.

The closing is the moment to land the argument:

  • "Based on these facts and this rule, the court must rule for the plaintiff because [strongest reason]."
  • "Based on these facts and this rule, the court should rule for the defendant because [strongest reason]."

7. Deliberation

The judge takes time to deliberate — even 2–3 minutes of visible thinking. Don't rush it. Real judges take days, weeks, or months. The student should feel the weight of the decision.

The judge can step out of the room briefly to think, then return.

8. The Ruling

The judge issues the ruling formally:

"The court has deliberated. The facts found are: [brief statement]. The rule applied is: [Charter provision]. The court rules that [plaintiff/defendant] prevails because [reasoning]. The remedy is: [whatever the court orders, which may or may not be what the plaintiff asked for]."

"The case is concluded. Both parties may request a review per the Charter's appeal provisions. Court is adjourned."

If the student loses, the facilitator-judge should reinforce: the trial was the success, not the win. They ran the procedure. They presented a case. They heard a ruling. That is real legal literacy in action.

If the Charter was silent or ambiguous on a key point, note that in the ruling. The judge can say:

"The court notes that the Charter does not explicitly address [this specific issue]. The court has ruled based on the apparent intent of [Rule X]. This is a gap that may need to be patched in a future amendment."

That's exactly how real courts often work — and it sets up Week 18's Amendment v2.0.

Independent Practice​

Goal​

Write the formal verdict — even though the trial is over — and reflect on running the proceeding.

Activities​

1. The Written Verdict

Even though the ruling was delivered orally, write it out formally:

═══════════════════════════════════════════════
RULING
[Plaintiff] v. [Defendant]
The [Name] Charter Court
═══════════════════════════════════════════════

FACTS FOUND:
[What the court determined actually happened]

CHARTER PROVISION APPLIED:
[Which rule controlled the case, quoted]

ANALYSIS:
[How the rule applies to the facts]

RULING:
[Who prevails, on what grounds]

REMEDY:
[What was ordered]

NOTES:
[Any gaps in the Charter the case exposed]

Date: ___________

The student writes this even if they were the plaintiff. That's the point — they have to put on the judge's hat and write the ruling as the system actually delivered it. This is the deepest exercise of the week.

2. The Post-Trial Reflection

Write down:

  • What surprised you about being in the proceeding?
  • Was there a moment when you knew which way the ruling would go?
  • If you had to do the trial over, what would you do differently?
  • Did the Charter handle this case well? Did any gaps show up?

Case Notes​

This week's Case Notes entry:

Date of trial:

The case (one sentence):

My role (plaintiff or defendant):

The ruling:

Did I win, lose, or partially win?

One thing I argued well:

One thing I'd argue better next time:

One gap in the Charter that the trial exposed:

One thing I now understand about how trials work that I didn't before:

Reflection Questions​

  • Was running the procedure harder or easier than you expected? Where was it hardest?
  • Did the Listening Protocol you wrote in Week 16 actually do its job?
  • Now that you've sat through one, do you have more or less respect for the people who do this for a living?

Check for Understanding​

After this week, check whether the learner can:

  1. Walk through the procedure: "Can you tell me the steps of the trial in order?" (Looking for: complaint → notice → evidence → opening → presentation → closing → deliberation → ruling.)
  2. Identify the parties and their roles: "What's the difference between a plaintiff and a defendant? What does the judge do?" (Looking for: brings the case / accused, decides.)
  3. Connect the trial to the Charter: "Show me which Charter provision was the heart of your case." (Looking for: ability to point to a specific rule and explain how it applied.)

If the learner can do 2 of 3 — and they ran the proceeding — they're ready for Week 18.

Pause and Notice​

What Matters Here

After the trial concludes, ask:

"What did it feel like to actually run a proceeding instead of just arguing?"

Most students notice something specific: the structure changed the conversation. They couldn't interrupt. They couldn't shout. They had to actually marshal their evidence and argue it. The same dispute, run informally, would have devolved into "yes you did" / "no I didn't" within minutes.

"The procedure is the thing that lets cases get resolved instead of just spiraling. Without it, you'd be having the same fight forever. With it, you reach a ruling — even if not everyone agrees with the ruling."

"Real trials can take weeks or months, but they're using the same skeleton you just used: complaint, evidence, arguments, deliberation, ruling. You've now done the actual thing legal systems are built around."

This week's takeaway: A trial is not just about winning or losing. It is the formal mechanism that converts a dispute into a resolution. The dispute might have been resolvable some other way — by talking, by mediation, by walking away — but when those fail, the trial is what's left. And it works because the procedure is in place before anyone needs to use it.

Spiral Review​

Connecting to Earlier Weeks
  • From Week 8 (Letter vs. Intent): When the Charter is ambiguous, the trial forces the court to choose between the literal reading and the intended one. You felt that in real time.
  • From Week 10 (Precedent): The ruling you got this week is now precedent for any similar future dispute in the domain. Treat it carefully.
  • From Week 13 (Micro-Charter): Every section of the Charter got tested. Rules were applied. Rights were invoked or weren't. Dispute resolution actually ran.
  • From Week 15 (How Courts Work): Jurisdiction (this court hears these cases), standing (the plaintiff was actually affected), and burden of proof (the plaintiff had to prove their claim) all showed up explicitly.
  • From Week 16 (Due Process): The Listening Protocol governed the trial. Every step you took was a step you wrote yourself last week.
Simplify (Ages 8–9)

Reduce the trial to: opening, plaintiff's story, defendant's story, judge's question, ruling. Five steps. Run the whole thing in 15 minutes. The structural lesson — both sides speak, judge decides, ruling has reasoning — comes through even at this scale.

Extend (Ages 10–12)

Have the older learner write the judge's full written opinion themselves as an exercise — even after the actual ruling. They have to articulate the legal reasoning, cite the Charter, address the strongest counter-argument, and conclude. This is functionally what a real appellate judge does.

Vocabulary This Week
  • Plaintiff — The party who brings a case to court.
  • Defendant — The party accused or sued in a case.
  • Complaint — The formal written document that starts a case.
  • Witness — Someone who provides direct knowledge of what happened.
  • Evidence — Anything that helps prove or disprove the facts in question.
  • Deliberation — The judge's process of weighing the case before issuing a ruling.
  • Verdict / Ruling — The formal decision in the case, with reasoning.

Preview of Next Week​

Next week is the finale. We deliver the formal verdict, introduce the concept of appeal — the idea that even a final ruling can be reviewed if the process was flawed — and you write the final upgrade to the Charter: Amendment v2.0. Then we close the curriculum with a reflection on what 18 weeks of legal literacy has actually given you.
