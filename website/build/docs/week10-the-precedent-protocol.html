Week 10: How Past Decisions Shape Future Ones

Debugging the Code — Phase 3 Finale: The Precedent Protocol

You are the judge this week.

A school posts a sign in the hallway. The sign reads: "NO RUNNING IN THE HALLWAYS." Then a series of cases come before you — eight of them — each one slightly weirder than the last. You have to rule on every one. And here's the catch:

Your earlier rulings become binding for your later rulings.

This is called precedent, and it is the version-control system of the legal world. Courts don't get to rewrite the entire codebase every time something tricky comes up. They look at past decisions and patch the law incrementally. This week, you'll feel exactly what that's like — and how a single early ruling can lock you into difficult later ones.

Facilitator Snapshot
  • The eight escalating cases are the spine of this week. Run them in order. Each case should feel slightly harder than the previous one.
  • The student MUST justify each ruling using their own earlier rulings as binding precedent. That constraint is the lesson.
  • Some students will want to overturn their early rulings as cases get harder. Allow it — but require them to articulate why (legal systems do this too; it's called "distinguishing" or "overturning" precedent).
  • This is the most pedagogically interesting activity in Unit 3. Plan to spend most of the week on it.

Week at a Glance​

Prep time~15 minutes
MaterialsPaper, pencil, a "ruling log" sheet, Case Notes
Key vocabularyprecedent, binding, distinguish, overturn, stare decisis
DifficultyAdvanced

Facilitator Preparation​

Before You Begin
  • Print or sketch a simple Ruling Log with these columns: Case #, Description, My Ruling, Reasoning, Cites (which earlier ruling controls this one).
  • Read through all 8 cases below so you can present them naturally.
  • Be ready to push back on rulings that contradict earlier ones. Your job is to be the "appellate judge" who points out inconsistencies.
Facilitation Mindset

The student's first ruling will feel easy and intuitive. By case 4 or 5, they'll start sweating, because their earlier rulings are constraining them in ways they didn't anticipate. That feeling is the lesson. Don't rescue them from it. Let them sit in the discomfort and reason their way through.

For Younger Learners (Ages 8–9)​

Adapting This Week

Simplest version of the concept: "When a judge decides one case, that decision becomes a rule for similar cases later. So you have to think carefully about every ruling, because it sticks."

What to shorten or skip:

  • Use 4 cases instead of 8.
  • Skip the "stare decisis" Latin term.
  • Keep sessions to 20 minutes.

Adapting the activities:

  • Have the student physically draw each case on a separate card.
  • Use stickers or stars to mark which earlier ruling controls each new case.

Journal alternative: Draw the four cases in a row, with arrows showing how each one connects to the earlier one.

What success looks like: The learner can explain why their decision on case 1 affected what they had to decide on case 4.

For Ages 10–12
  • Introduce the Latin phrase stare decisis ("to stand by decided things") — the formal principle of precedent.
  • Discuss when a court should distinguish a new case from an old one ("this case is different in this important way, so the precedent doesn't quite apply") versus overturn the old precedent ("our previous ruling was wrong, we're changing it").
  • Have older learners write a brief "judicial opinion" for each ruling.

Guided Session 1​

The First Ruling Locks You In​

Learning Goal​

By the end of this session, the student can:

  • act as a judge ruling on a series of escalating cases
  • recognize that each ruling constrains future rulings
  • begin to articulate when their own precedent forces a difficult later decision

Activities​

1. The Rule and the Setup

Read the rule aloud:

"NO RUNNING IN THE HALLWAYS."

"You are the principal of this school. You are also the judge. Anyone who is accused of breaking this rule gets brought to you. You have to rule: did they violate the rule, yes or no? And whatever you decide becomes a precedent — meaning if a similar case comes up later, you have to be consistent."

Hand the student the Ruling Log sheet. Explain:

"Every ruling, you'll write: (1) the case, (2) your decision, (3) your reasoning. Later cases will refer back to your earlier decisions. Try to be consistent — or be very clear about why you're changing your mind."

2. Run the Cases — Part 1

Present these one at a time. The student rules on each. The facilitator should write the ruling on the log and refer back to it for the next case.

Case 1: A child is sprinting at full speed down the hallway, almost knocking over another student.

(This is the obvious "core" case. Almost every student will rule: yes, violates the rule.)

After they rule, have them write the reasoning:

"Sprinting at full speed is clearly running. The rule applies."

Case 2: A child is moving very fast down the hallway but insists they are "speed-walking," not running. Their feet stay in contact with the floor.

(Now the student has to decide: is "running" defined by speed, or by gait, or both?)

Push them: "What does your Case 1 ruling tell us about how 'running' is being defined? Was it the speed that mattered, or the running motion?"

Have them write their ruling and reasoning. Whatever they decide here becomes important.

Case 3: A child is in a wheelchair, propelling themselves at jogging speed using their arms.

(Wheelchairs are mobility equipment. Are they running?)

Watch for: did their Case 2 ruling tie the rule to speed? If yes, the wheelchair might violate. If they tied it to "running motion," wheelchairs don't violate.

The student has to reason from precedent.

Case 4: A teacher is sprinting down the hallway because they heard a fight breaking out and need to stop it.

(The classic intent-vs.-letter case from Week 8. The letter says no running. But the purpose of running is safety here.)

How does the student handle this? Common rulings:

  • "The rule still applies, but I'd dismiss the punishment because of the circumstance." (One approach.)
  • "The rule contains an implicit emergency exception." (Another approach.)
  • "The teacher technically violated it." (A strict-letter approach.)

Whatever they choose becomes precedent for Case 5.

Guided Session 2​

Distinguishing and Overturning​

Learning Goal​

By the end of this session, the student can:

  • continue ruling on cases that test their earlier precedents
  • recognize when a new case can be "distinguished" from an earlier one
  • understand when a precedent should be overturned, and what that means

Activities​

1. Run the Cases — Part 2

Continuing the case sequence:

Case 5: A fire drill is in progress. Everyone is moving quickly toward the exit, including some running.

(Group case. The intent is safety. The letter is clear violation.)

Did the student's ruling on Case 4 (the teacher running to a fight) create an emergency exception? If yes, this case fits the exception. If no — this case forces them to either create the exception now, or apply the rule absurdly.

Case 6: A child is riding a small scooter down the hallway. Their feet aren't running — they're just rolling fast.

(Wheels again, like Case 3. Is a scooter "running"?)

The student must reconcile this with their wheelchair ruling. If the wheelchair was allowed (because not "running"), the scooter should probably be allowed too. If the wheelchair was banned (because of speed), the scooter should be too.

This is where students start feeling the constraining power of precedent. They might want to ban the scooter because it feels different from a wheelchair — but their earlier reasoning ties their hands.

Case 7: A child is hopping on one foot down the hallway, very quickly.

(Not running in the technical sense. But the speed and gait look similar.)

Now the student has to decide: is "running" about speed, or about the running motion, or both? And their answer must be consistent with their earlier cases.

Case 8: A child is running down the hallway while inside a giant inflatable hamster ball. They are not technically touching the floor; the ball is.

(The fun absurd case. The kid's feet aren't on the floor. They're running in place inside a rolling ball.)

Whatever they decide here, push them on it: "Why do you rule that way? What's the principle? Could anyone make sense of your collected rulings?"

2. The Distinguishing Move

If the student feels stuck — like an earlier ruling is forcing a result that feels wrong — introduce distinguishing:

"A real judge has a tool. If a new case seems to violate your old precedent in a way that feels wrong, you can argue that the new case is different from the old one in a way that matters. That's called distinguishing a precedent."

"For example: in Case 6, you might say, 'Wheelchairs serve mobility — they're a person's legs. Scooters are recreational — they're an extra speed boost. Even though both have wheels, they're meaningfully different, so my wheelchair ruling doesn't control this case.'"

Distinguishing is honest. It says, "I'm following precedent in spirit, but I'm noticing that this case is genuinely new."

3. The Overturning Move

The other tool is more dramatic:

"Sometimes a judge looks at their own earlier ruling and says, 'Actually, I was wrong. The rule I made then doesn't work. I'm going to change it.' That's called overturning a precedent."

"Overturning is a bigger deal than distinguishing. It says: 'Anyone who relied on the old ruling can't anymore.' It's like a major code change that breaks earlier patches."

Ask the student: were any of their earlier rulings ones they'd now overturn?

If yes, have them write: "I overturn my ruling on Case X because [reason]. Going forward, the rule should be [new ruling]."

Independent Practice​

Goal​

Apply precedent thinking to rules in the student's actual life.

Activities​

1. The Precedent Audit

Pick one rule from the student's actual life (home, school, etc.). Now imagine the rule has been applied in different cases over time, and ask:

  • Has anyone in your house ever ruled on this rule's application before?
  • Was the ruling consistent across different family members? Different days? Different moods?
  • Does the rule actually have "precedent," or does it get reinterpreted from scratch every time?

This is one of the deepest things to notice about real-world rule-following: most household rules are NOT enforced like real precedent. They get re-decided based on the enforcer's mood, the time of day, who's involved. That inconsistency is what people often complain about when a rule "isn't fair."

2. The Inconsistency Hunt

Find one example in your real life where the same rule has been applied differently in similar situations. Write:

  • The rule.
  • Two situations that seemed similar.
  • How they were treated differently.
  • What that inconsistency felt like.

This is the lived experience of precedent failure. Real legal systems try to prevent it. Real households often don't.

Case Notes​

Add this to your Case Notes:

Date:

My Ruling Log — all 8 cases: 1. 2. 3. 4. 5. 6. 7. 8.

The ruling that locked me in most painfully later — and how:

One case I had to distinguish from an earlier one:

A real-life rule in my house that has 'precedent problems' — meaning, it gets applied inconsistently:

Sentence starters for younger learners:

  • "When I said yes to ___, that made me have to say yes to ___."
  • "When I said no to ___, that made me have to say no to ___."

Reflection Questions​

  • Did your rulings get easier or harder as you went? Why?
  • If you started the cases over from Case 1, knowing what you know now, would you rule differently?
  • When real courts get cases that have built up many layers of precedent, do you think they have more freedom or less freedom than a court ruling on a brand-new question?

Check for Understanding​

After this week, check whether the learner can:

  1. Define precedent in their own words: "What is precedent?" (Looking for: a past ruling that becomes binding for future similar cases.)
  2. Show how a precedent locked them in: "Tell me about a moment when your earlier ruling forced you into a harder later one." (Looking for: any honest example from the case sequence.)
  3. Use 'distinguish' or 'overturn' correctly: "What's the difference between distinguishing a precedent and overturning it?" (Looking for: distinguishing = this case is different; overturning = the old ruling was wrong.)

If the learner can do at least 2 of these, they're ready for Week 11.

Pause and Notice​

What Matters Here

After running all 8 cases, ask:

"Did being the judge feel powerful or constraining?"

Most students say both. The judge gets to decide. But every decision narrows what they can decide next.

"Real judges sometimes deal with precedents that are 100 years old. They might personally disagree with the old ruling. But if they overturn it, every business and family and school that relied on the old ruling gets disrupted. So they often follow precedent even when they don't love it."

This is one of the deepest tensions in any legal system. Stability is a value all its own. Even an imperfect ruling can be valuable if everyone has built their lives around it. Changing it has costs.

"If you were a judge, would you rather follow an old precedent you slightly disagreed with, or overturn it?"

Don't try to settle this. Let the student feel the weight.

This week's takeaway: Precedent is how legal systems learn over time. Each ruling is both a decision and a future constraint. Good judges think about both the case in front of them and the cases that will come next — because their ruling is now part of the code.

Spiral Review​

Connecting to Earlier Weeks

From Week 8: "No vehicles in the park" had the same ambiguity problem as "no running in the hallways." Notice that the precedent protocol is one way legal systems handle ambiguity over time — by accumulating rulings about edge cases.

From Week 9: Loopholes happen when a rule's words can be gamed. Precedent can close loopholes by ruling against them: once a judge has ruled that "speed-walking" violates "no running," the loophole is dead.

Simplify (Ages 8–9)

Run only 4 cases (sprinting, speed-walking, scooter, hamster ball — skip the harder ones). The point is to feel the constraint of an earlier ruling on a later one. Four cases is enough for that.

Extend (Ages 10–12)

After running the cases, have older learners write a one-paragraph judicial opinion for one of their rulings. The opinion should: (a) state the facts, (b) cite any earlier ruling that controls it, (c) explain the reasoning, (d) state the holding.

Vocabulary This Week
  • Precedent — A past ruling that becomes binding for similar future cases.
  • Binding — Required to be followed. A binding precedent constrains future decisions.
  • Distinguish — To argue that a new case is meaningfully different from an old precedent, so the precedent doesn't apply.
  • Overturn — To replace an old precedent with a new ruling. A bigger move than distinguishing.
  • Stare decisis — Latin for "to stand by decided things." The principle that courts follow precedent.
