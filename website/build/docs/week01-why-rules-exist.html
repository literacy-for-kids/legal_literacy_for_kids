Week 1: Starting at Zero: The Need for Law

The Architecture of Rules — Phase 1

Welcome to Legal Literacy.

Over the next 18 weeks, we are going to look at the legal system the way a software engineer looks at an operating system: a giant set of rules running in the background that makes everything else possible. Most of the time, when the system is working, you don't even notice it. You stop at a red light without thinking. You hand a cashier money and walk out with a sandwich. You sit in a classroom and know roughly what's allowed and what isn't.

This week, we are going to turn it off in our heads — imagine a world with zero rules — and watch what happens. The point is not to scare anyone. The point is to discover, by feel, why rules are not arbitrary. They exist because without them, the system crashes within hours.

Facilitator Snapshot
  • The big move this week is letting the learner feel what life would be like with no rules at all, then articulate why that would be unworkable.
  • Resist the urge to explain "why rules are important" up front. Let the thought experiment do the work.
  • Introduce Case Notes at the end of the week. A plain notebook or folder is all you need — it will travel with the student through all 18 weeks.
  • Keep the tone curious and a little playful. Law is more interesting than most kids expect, and the first week sets the vibe.

Week at a Glance​

Prep time~10 minutes
MaterialsPaper, pencil, sticky notes or index cards, a plain notebook for Case Notes
Key vocabularylaw, rule, system, predictability, scale
DifficultyIntroductory

Facilitator Preparation​

Before You Begin
  • Read through both Guided Sessions and pick which day you'll do each one.
  • Prepare a notebook or folder labeled Case Notes — this is the journal for the full course.
  • Have ~10 sticky notes or index cards ready for the rule-sorting activity in Session 2.
  • Pick one rule from your own household you can use as a real example ("we take shoes off at the door," "no phones at dinner," etc.).
Facilitation Mindset

The goal this week is noticing, not memorizing. The student already lives inside a thick web of rules — they just don't see it yet. Your job is to point at the air and say, "look, you've been breathing this whole time."

Be careful not to use the words "should" and "must" too much. Treat rules as objects of curiosity, not commands.

For Younger Learners (Ages 8–9)​

Adapting This Week

Simplest version of the concept: "Rules are agreements that let lots of people share the same world without crashing into each other."

What to shorten or skip:

  • Skip the "predictability" vocabulary word. Just talk about "knowing what's going to happen."
  • In the No Rules Thought Experiment, keep it to one location (school OR home, not both).
  • Keep sessions to 20 minutes.

Adapting the activities:

  • For the rule sort, use 5–6 rules instead of 10.
  • Accept drawings instead of written entries in Case Notes.

Journal alternative: A drawing labeled "Three Rules in My House" with arrows pointing to each one. Or one spoken sentence: "A rule I follow is ___."

What success looks like: The learner can name three rules they follow and give a simple reason for each one.

For Ages 10–12
  • Push them to articulate the difference between a rule someone gave them ("brush your teeth") and a rule they invented for themselves ("I always finish my homework before screen time").
  • Introduce the word predictability explicitly and discuss why it matters when many people are involved.
  • Have them spot at least one "rule" that is actually a custom rather than a law (e.g., taking shoes off at the door isn't legally required, but most households treat it as a real rule).

Guided Session 1​

A Day Without Rules​

Learning Goal​

By the end of this session, the student can:

  • describe what would happen if all rules were suddenly suspended for a day
  • explain why rules exist as a structural condition, not just a parent or teacher being bossy
  • begin to see rules as a system that runs in the background

Activities​

1. The Thought Experiment

Ask:

"Imagine you wake up tomorrow and overnight, every single rule has been turned off. No school rules. No laws. No promises. No traffic lights. Nothing. What happens in the first hour of your day?"

Let the student narrate. Listen carefully — they will usually start with small things (no bedtime, can eat cookies for breakfast) before they realize the bigger consequences (no one drives safely, no one shows up to work, no one delivers the food to the store).

Steer the conversation toward escalation:

  • "Okay, you ate cookies for breakfast. Now your parent wants to drive you somewhere. What happens at the first intersection?"
  • "Imagine the store. The shelves are empty because the trucks didn't come. Why didn't the trucks come?"
  • "Now imagine someone takes your bike. There's no rule that says they can't. What can you do?"

The point is to walk the student up the chain from inconvenience to system collapse.

2. The Big Idea

After they've sat with the thought experiment, name the idea:

"Most of the time, you don't notice rules because they're working. They run in the background, like an operating system on a computer. You only notice them when they break."

"Rules exist because if too many people are doing too many things at once, the whole system needs predictability to work. Predictability is just a fancy word for 'knowing roughly what's going to happen.'"

Ask:

"Why does knowing what's going to happen actually matter?"

Help them land on: because you can plan, you can trust other people, and you don't have to fight over every little thing.

3. The Operating System Analogy

If the student has any experience with computers or games, ask:

"Have you ever had a game crash or freeze? What happened to the rules of the game in that moment?"

The rules disappeared. Until the game was reloaded, nothing worked. That is what a society without law looks like — except you can't just close it and reopen it.

If the student doesn't have a tech reference, use a board game: "What happens to Monopoly if no one agrees on the rules?" (Answer: it stops being Monopoly. It's just shouting around a board.)

Guided Session 2​

Rules You Already Run​

Learning Goal​

By the end of this session, the student can:

  • identify rules they already follow without thinking
  • sort rules by what they are for (safety, fairness, coordination, etc.)
  • notice the difference between rules from authority and rules from custom

Activities​

1. The Rule Inventory

Together, brainstorm rules the student already follows. Write each one on a separate sticky note or index card. Aim for 10 rules from at least four different settings:

SettingExample Rule
Home"Shoes off at the door"
School"Raise your hand before speaking"
Road"Stop at a red light"
Store"Wait in line to pay"
Online"Don't share your password"
Sports"Touch base before you score"

If the student gets stuck, offer prompts: "What's a rule about food? About money? About what time you go to bed?"

2. Sort the Rules

Lay the sticky notes on the table. Ask:

"Can you group these into piles? You decide the categories."

Let them sort freely first. Then introduce three buckets:

SafetyFairnessCoordination
Stop the system from hurting peopleMake sure no one gets cheatedLet many people do the same thing without crashing

Re-sort the rules into these categories. Some will fit in more than one bucket — that's a great discussion. ("Raise your hand" is fairness AND coordination.)

3. Where Did Each Rule Come From?

For each rule, ask:

"Did someone tell you this rule, or did you just absorb it?"

Distinguish:

  • Laws — written down by a government, with consequences enforced by official systems (e.g., stopping at a red light)
  • Institutional rules — written down by a school, store, sports league, or workplace (e.g., raise your hand)
  • Customs — unwritten but everyone follows them anyway (e.g., shoes off at the door)
  • Promises and contracts — agreements between specific people (e.g., "I'll feed the dog every morning")

Notice that the student already lives in a thick stack of rules of all four kinds, most of which they've never thought about as "rules."

Independent Practice​

Goal​

Notice the rules already running in the student's life, and start the Case Notes journal that will travel with them through all 18 weeks.

Activities​

1. The Rule Scavenger Hunt

Over one day, the student keeps a small running list of every rule they notice themselves following. Aim for 15 rules. Don't filter — write down even the small ones ("I knocked before opening the bathroom door").

At the end of the day, review the list together and tag each one as L (law), I (institutional), C (custom), or P (promise).

2. Why Does This Rule Exist?

Pick three rules from the scavenger hunt list. For each one, write one sentence answering:

"What would happen if no one followed this rule?"

Examples:

  • "If no one stopped at red lights, cars would crash and people would get hurt."
  • "If no one knocked on the bathroom door, there would be a lot of awkward moments."
  • "If no one waited in line, the strongest or rudest person would always go first."

Case Notes​

This is your first entry in Case Notes, the running journal you'll keep for the next 18 weeks. Think of it as your logbook of observations about the rules and systems around you.

For this first entry, write:

Date:

Three rules I noticed today: 1. 2. 3.

One rule that, if everyone ignored it, would cause real problems:

One rule that seems weird, unnecessary, or unfair:

A question I have about rules:

Sentence starters for younger learners:

  • "A rule I follow is ___."
  • "If no one followed it, ___ would happen."
  • "A weird rule is ___."

Oral or drawn option: Draw three rules and label them. Or describe them out loud and have the facilitator write them down.

Reflection Questions​

  • Which rule on your list surprised you when you wrote it down? Why?
  • Are there rules you follow even though no one is watching? Why?
  • Is there a rule that seems unfair to you? What would you change about it?

Check for Understanding​

After this week, check whether the learner can:

  1. Explain in one sentence: "Why do rules exist?" (Looking for: anything about safety, fairness, coordination, predictability, or letting many people share the same world.)
  2. Spot a real rule: "Tell me one rule you followed today without thinking about it." (Looking for: any specific example.)
  3. Predict consequences: "What would happen if no one stopped at red lights?" (Looking for: crashes, chaos, danger — they understand consequences propagate.)

If the learner can do at least 2 of these, they're ready for Week 2.

Pause and Notice​

What Matters Here

After Session 2, ask:

"Is there a rule on your list that you don't like? One that feels unfair or pointless?"

Take their answer seriously. Don't argue. Just listen.

Then ask:

"If you were in charge, what would you change about it? Would you remove it, or change it?"

This is the first time in the curriculum we plant the idea that rules are designed objects — meaning someone made them, and they can be unmade or improved. We are not training rule-followers. We are training rule-readers who can ask whether each rule is doing its job.

This week's takeaway: Rules are not random. They exist for reasons. Once you can see them, you can ask whether each one is working — and that is a real superpower.

Spiral Review​

Connecting to Earlier Weeks

This is Week 1, so there's nothing earlier to connect back to. But here's a forward-looking note: every week from here on, this section will reference what you've already learned. Pay attention — by Week 18, you will have built a real toolkit, and Spiral Review is how we make sure none of it gets forgotten.

Simplify (Ages 8–9)

If the learner is younger, skip the four-way sort (Law / Institutional / Custom / Promise). Just sort rules into "things that keep us safe" and "things that make things fair." The single key idea — rules exist for reasons — is enough for the first week.

Extend (Ages 10–12)

Challenge older learners to find a rule that exists for a reason no one currently remembers. (Hint: many customs are like this. Why do we shake hands? Why do judges wear robes?) Discuss: does a rule need a current good reason to be worth keeping?

Vocabulary This Week
  • Law — A rule written down by a government, with official consequences for breaking it.
  • Rule — An instruction that tells people what to do or not do.
  • System — A collection of parts that work together. The legal system is the collection of laws, courts, and procedures that work together.
  • Predictability — Knowing roughly what's going to happen next. Rules create predictability.
  • Scale — The number of people a system has to handle. Rules become more important as the scale gets bigger.
