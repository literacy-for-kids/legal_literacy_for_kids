Week 11: What the System Cannot Do to You

The Governance Project — Phase 4 Begins

Most rules are about what you can't do to other people.

This week, we look at the other side: what the system can't do to you.

These are called rights, and they are some of the most important objects in any legal system. A right is not a permission. A right is not a feeling. A right is a firewall — a hardcoded constraint on what the system is allowed to do to an individual, even when the majority wants to.

This is hard to grasp at first because it pushes back on something we've been taught: that "the majority wins." Sometimes the majority can't win. There are some things the system is just not allowed to do, no matter how many votes are on the other side. That's what a right is.

Facilitator Snapshot
  • The big move this week: a right is a constraint on institutional power, not a personal feeling. "I have a right to my opinion" is a common but imprecise use of the word.
  • The key insight: rights only matter when they protect the unpopular person. Rights protecting the popular don't get tested.
  • This week introduces the Micro-Charter as the coming capstone. From here on, every week references the Charter you'll write in Week 13.
  • Be careful not to wade into political controversies. Use neutral, structural examples.

Prep time~15 minutes
MaterialsPaper, pencil, Case Notes, the student's Three Laws from Week 3
Key vocabularyright, firewall, unalienable, majority, individual node
DifficultyAdvanced

Facilitator Preparation​

Before You Begin
  • Pull out the student's Three Laws from Week 3 — we'll examine whether any of them protect individuals from the group.
  • Read both Sessions and prepare to use neutral examples (procedural rights, not policy controversies).
  • Tease the upcoming Micro-Charter (Week 13) so the student knows this unit is building toward something real.
Facilitation Mindset

This is one of the more philosophically heavy weeks. Use concrete examples wherever possible. Avoid abstract political fights. The goal is for the student to understand the structural concept of a right, not to litigate any specific debate.

For Younger Learners (Ages 8–9)​

Adapting This Week

Simplest version of the concept: "Some things are not allowed to be taken away from you, even if everyone else votes that they should be. Those things are called rights."

What to shorten or skip:

  • Skip the "firewall" analogy if it's too abstract. Use "force field" or "shield."
  • Skip the majority/minority discussion at the abstract level. Use only concrete examples.
  • Keep sessions to 20 minutes.

Adapting the activities:

  • Use very concrete examples (your right to not be hit, your right to keep your own diary private).
  • Accept drawings of "shields" around the things rights protect.

Journal alternative: Draw three "shields" around three things the student thinks should be protected from being taken away by a vote.

What success looks like: The learner can name one thing that should be protected even if everyone else voted to take it away.

For Ages 10–12
  • Introduce the distinction between positive rights (the system must provide something — like education, in some countries) and negative rights (the system must not do something — like search you without reason).
  • Discuss why rights tend to be written down at the founding of a system, not later. Why is it harder to add new rights once a system is running?
  • Have older learners examine the Bill of Rights (or their country's foundational rights document) and identify which rights are firewalls against majority decisions.

Guided Session 1​

The Firewall Concept​

Learning Goal​

By the end of this session, the student can:

  • define a right as a constraint on institutional power, not just a personal preference
  • explain why rights exist as firewalls against majority action
  • distinguish rights from other kinds of rules

Activities​

1. The Voting Thought Experiment

Set the scene:

"Imagine your class has 25 kids. Everyone votes on what game to play at recess. Soccer gets 24 votes. Tag gets 1 vote. They play soccer. The majority wins. That feels fair."

"Now imagine the same class votes on a new rule: '[One specific student's name] must give their lunch to the rest of us every day.' 24 vote yes. The student votes no. The majority wins. The student is forced to hand over their lunch."

"Does that feel fair?"

Most students immediately say no. Push:

"Why not? The majority won. Same voting system as the soccer choice."

The student usually lands on: "Because that's not the kind of thing you can vote on. You can't vote to take someone's food just because more of you want it."

That intuition is the beginning of understanding rights. Some things are not the kind of things that majorities are allowed to decide.

2. The Firewall

Drop the analogy:

"In a computer system, a firewall is a hardcoded barrier. Even if every other part of the system requests something, the firewall can block it. The firewall doesn't care about majority opinion. It's there to prevent specific bad outcomes no matter what."

"Rights work the same way. They are hardcoded barriers that protect the individual from being overridden by the system — even when the system has a clear majority pushing for it."

Examples of rights as firewalls (use neutral, procedural ones — not policy-divisive ones):

  • The right not to be punished without being told why. Even if 99% of the people in your school think you broke a rule, the school still has to tell you what rule and give you a chance to respond.
  • The right not to have your private things searched without reason. Even if the principal really wants to look in your backpack, they're not supposed to do it without a real cause.
  • The right to remain silent when accused. Even if everyone expects you to defend yourself, you can choose not to speak — and that choice can't be used against you.
  • The right to fair treatment regardless of who you are. The rules have to be applied the same way to everyone, not differently based on who's popular.

Notice: these are all constraints on the institution, not permissions for the individual. A right is a limit on power.

3. What's NOT a Right

This is the tricky part. People sometimes use the word "right" loosely. Sort each of these into RIGHT (a constraint on institutional power) or NOT A RIGHT (something else):

  • "I have a right to my own opinion." → Not a legal right in the firewall sense. It's a feeling about freedom of thought, which actually IS related to a real right (freedom of conscience), but the phrasing is loose.
  • "I have a right to a free phone." → Not a right in most legal systems. Phones aren't protected. (This use of "right" usually means "I want / I think I deserve.")
  • "I have a right to a lawyer if I'm accused of a crime." → A real right in most countries. It constrains what the system can do to you.
  • "I have a right to be liked by my classmates." → Not a right. No one can force people to like you. (Though you do have a right to not be physically harmed by them — that's different.)
  • "I have a right to be treated equally under the law." → A foundational right.
  • "I have a right to never feel sad." → Not a right. Feelings aren't legally protectable.

Pattern: real rights are constraints on what institutions can do to you. They're not guarantees of outcomes or feelings.

Guided Session 2​

When Rights Get Tested​

Learning Goal​

By the end of this session, the student can:

  • recognize that rights only matter when they protect unpopular people or actions
  • identify situations where the majority would want to override a right
  • explain why rights are written into foundational documents and made hard to change

Activities​

1. The Unpopular Test

Walk through this carefully:

"Imagine a town has a right written into its laws: 'Every person has the right to read whatever books they choose.' One day, someone in town reads a book that almost everyone hates. The town gathers and votes: '90% of us want this person banned from reading that book.'"

"Does the right protect that person?"

Yes. That's exactly when rights matter most.

"Now imagine the same right, but everyone is reading popular books. No one's reading anything controversial. Does the right matter on those days?"

The right is technically there, but it doesn't have to do any work. Rights only earn their keep when they protect the unpopular case.

Drop the insight:

"If a right only protects you when the majority already wants you protected, it's not really a right — it's just a description of what the majority wants. A right is only real when it can block the majority from doing something it wants to do."

This is one of the most counter-intuitive ideas in this curriculum. Pause on it.

2. Looking at Real Rights

Look at any list of foundational rights (in the U.S., the Bill of Rights; in other countries, your equivalent foundational document). Find 3–4 specific rights and ask, for each:

  • What does this right prevent the system from doing?
  • When does this right matter most — when is the system tempted to violate it?
  • What would happen without this right?

Example: "The government cannot force you to be a witness against yourself."

  • What it prevents: The system can't punish you for refusing to answer questions about your own guilt.
  • When it matters most: When you're actually accused of something, and everyone wants you to confess.
  • Without it: People would be forced to incriminate themselves under pressure, even if they were innocent.

Notice that every right has this structure: prohibition + protection of a specific situation.

3. Foreshadow the Micro-Charter

This is the moment to tease the upcoming capstone:

"In two weeks, you're going to write a foundational document for a domain you choose — your bedroom, your friend group, a Minecraft server, anything. It's called the Micro-Charter, and it will include three things:"

"(1) Explicit rules about how the system works. (2) Unalienable rights — things that even a majority vote cannot override. (3) A protocol for resolving disputes."

"Right now, start thinking: in your domain, what are the rights? What are the things that even a majority shouldn't be able to overrule?"

Have the student write a preliminary list of 3–4 candidate rights for their (still undecided) Micro-Charter. These are notes, not final.

Independent Practice​

Goal​

Begin identifying the rights that should exist in the student's real domains.

Activities​

1. Rights in My Own Life

Pick three domains: home, school, and a friend group (or sibling group, sports team, etc.). For each, write:

  • What's a right I think every person in this domain should have?
  • What would happen if someone tried to vote it away?
  • Has this right ever been tested? (E.g., was there a moment when most people wanted to override it?)

Examples:

  • Home: "Everyone has the right to private space in their own bedroom." If everyone voted to take my sister's privacy away, that shouldn't work.
  • School: "Everyone has the right to be told why they're in trouble before being punished." Even if the teacher is sure, they should still say what the accusation is.
  • Friend group: "No one can be voted out of the group without being given a chance to respond to whatever the issue is."

2. The Domain Choice

This is where the student starts thinking about their Micro-Charter domain. They should commit to a domain by the end of this week. Options:

  • Their bedroom (especially if shared with a sibling)
  • A friend group
  • A Minecraft / Roblox / Discord server they run or participate in
  • A sports team or club
  • A family meeting structure
  • A book club or game night

The domain should be real — meaning the student actually participates in it. The Charter they write will be used.

Case Notes​

Add this to your Case Notes:

Date:

My definition of a right (in my own words):

One right I think should exist in my home:

One right I think should exist in my friend group or other domain:

My Micro-Charter domain — what I'll be writing the Charter for in Week 13:

Three candidate rights for my Micro-Charter: 1. 2. 3.

Sentence starters for younger learners:

  • "A right is ___."
  • "Even if everyone voted, no one should be able to ___."
  • "My Charter will be for ___."

Reflection Questions​

  • Have you ever been on the wrong side of a majority — when most people wanted something you didn't want? Was there a "right" that protected you, or did you just have to go along with it?
  • Why do you think rights are written down at the beginning of a system rather than added later?
  • If you could add one brand-new right to your school or country, what would it be?

Check for Understanding​

After this week, check whether the learner can:

  1. Define a right correctly: "What is a right?" (Looking for: a constraint on institutional power; something the system cannot do to you; protected even from majority decisions.)
  2. Distinguish a right from a preference: "Is 'I have a right to ice cream' a real right?" (Looking for: no — that's a preference, not a constraint on institutional power.)
  3. Name when rights matter: "When do rights matter most — when the majority agrees with you, or when it doesn't?" (Looking for: when the majority disagrees. Rights only do work when they protect the unpopular case.)

If the learner can do at least 2 of these, they're ready for Week 12.

Pause and Notice​

What Matters Here

After Session 2, ask:

"Imagine being the person whose right is being violated. Most people are against you. The system has been told they can take your right away. How does that feel?"

This is one of the more sobering moments in the curriculum. Rights only matter for the person being protected. From outside, a right looks like "the majority can't have what it wants." From inside the protected position, a right looks like the only thing standing between you and a system that wants to flatten you.

"Now imagine being on the majority side. You want something. The right blocks you. How does that feel?"

Frustrating. Often, the people who most want to override rights are the people in the majority who feel the right is in their way. That's the design challenge of a legal system: rights have to feel binding even when they're inconvenient to the powerful.

This week's takeaway: A right is only a real right when it can protect the unpopular case. If it only protects the popular, it's not doing the job rights are designed to do.

Spiral Review​

Connecting to Earlier Weeks

From Week 3: When you wrote your Three Laws for the island, did any of them function as rights — protecting individuals from the group? Look back. If not, would you add one now?

From Week 10: Precedent makes rules predictable over time. Rights make some things predictable across all possible majorities. Both are stability mechanisms, working on different time scales.

Simplify (Ages 8–9)

Skip the institutional-power vocabulary. Just teach: "A right is something that's protected even if everyone else votes against it." Use the "lunch vote" thought experiment as the anchor.

Extend (Ages 10–12)

Discuss the philosophical question: where do rights come from? Some thinkers say they're invented by societies and written down. Others say they exist before any document — humans have them by virtue of being human, and documents just record them. What does the student think? This is a real debate among legal philosophers.

Vocabulary This Week
  • Right — A constraint on institutional power that protects an individual, even from majority decisions.
  • Firewall — A hardcoded barrier in a system. Rights act as firewalls between institutions and individuals.
  • Unalienable — Cannot be taken away or transferred, even with the person's consent. Often used to describe foundational rights.
  • Majority — The larger side in a group vote. Majorities usually win, but rights block them on protected issues.
  • Individual node — In the network analogy, a single person in the system. Rights protect each node from being overwritten by the collective.

Preview of Next Week​

Next week, we look at distributed power — the design principle that prevents any single point in a system from being able to override everyone else. Single points of failure are bad in software, and they're bad in governance. We'll see how checks and balances work as fault tolerance, and start adding distributed power to your Micro-Charter.
