Week 4: The Anatomy of an Enforceable Promise

The Anatomy of a Promise — Phase 2 Begins

Imagine you tell your friend, "I'll give you my Pokémon card if you give me your sticker." They say yes. You hand them the card. They hand you the sticker.

You just made a contract.

Not "a sort of agreement." Not "a kind of deal." A contract. With all three required components, fully formed, legally complete (well — under most countries' contract law).

This week we open up the hood on contracts and look at the engine. We are going to learn that a contract is an interface specification — a precise list of inputs, outputs, and exchanges that turns a vague promise into a binding agreement. And we'll discover that almost every promise that doesn't become a contract is missing one of three specific parts.

Facilitator Snapshot
  • The big takeaway this week: a contract has exactly three required components — Offer, Acceptance, and Consideration. Miss any one, and you don't have a contract.
  • Students should leave this week able to look at any promise and ask: "Does it have all three parts?"
  • Don't get into the weeds of contract law subtleties (good faith, capacity, etc.). This week is the basic anatomy lesson.
  • We are setting up Weeks 5, 6, and 7. Each component gets its own week, and Week 7 is the practical capstone (a real contract).

Week at a Glance​

Prep time~10 minutes
MaterialsPaper, pencil, index cards, Case Notes
Key vocabularycontract, offer, acceptance, consideration, binding
DifficultyModerate

Facilitator Preparation​

Before You Begin
  • Read through both Guided Sessions.
  • Prepare 6–8 short "promise" scenarios on index cards (examples in Session 2).
  • Have a real contract or signed document on hand if possible — even a permission slip works. Students get more out of seeing the actual thing.
Facilitation Mindset

The temptation will be to teach contracts as serious adult things with scary language. Resist it. Contracts are everywhere. The student has already made dozens. The lesson is making the structure visible, not making it intimidating.

For Younger Learners (Ages 8–9)​

Adapting This Week

Simplest version of the concept: "A real deal has three parts: someone offers something, someone agrees, and both people give something. If any part is missing, it's not a real deal yet."

What to shorten or skip:

  • Skip the "interface specification" framing. Use "the three parts of a deal."
  • Use food and toy examples rather than money examples.
  • Keep sessions to 20 minutes.

Adapting the activities:

  • Act out the contracts physically. "Offer," "Acceptance," and "Consideration" can be three different hand gestures.
  • Accept drawings of contracts.

Journal alternative: Draw a contract you made (or could make) with someone in your house.

What success looks like: The learner can name the three parts of a contract and act out a simple example.

For Ages 10–12
  • Introduce the API specification analogy explicitly. A contract specifies inputs, outputs, and exchanges, just like a function signature does in code.
  • Discuss situations where a promise has all three parts but still isn't enforceable (e.g., a contract to do something illegal). Tease the idea that contracts also need legal subject matter.
  • Have them spot contracts in their daily life that they didn't know they were making (clicking "I agree" on a website, accepting an allowance, agreeing to chores).

Guided Session 1​

Three Required Parts​

Learning Goal​

By the end of this session, the student can:

  • name the three components of a contract: Offer, Acceptance, Consideration
  • recognize a contract in a real-world situation
  • identify when one of the three parts is missing

Activities​

1. The Pokémon Card Trade

Walk through this simple example, slowly:

"You say to your friend: 'I will give you my holographic Charizard if you give me your shiny Pikachu sticker.' That sentence is your offer. It says exactly what you'll give and what you want."

"Your friend says: 'Deal!' That's the acceptance. They've agreed to the offer. Now both of you know the trade is on."

"Then you hand over the Charizard. They hand over the Pikachu. That exchange — the actual giving — is called consideration. Each of you is giving something the other person values."

Stop and say:

"Boom. You just made a contract. Not a kid version. An actual, legally binding contract under most countries' contract law."

If the student looks surprised, lean in:

"This isn't a metaphor. Contract law really does work this way. The Pokémon trade has exactly the same structural parts as a multi-million-dollar business deal."

2. The Three Components Up Close

Write on a piece of paper:

OFFER: "I will do X if you do Y." ACCEPTANCE: "Yes, deal." CONSIDERATION: Both sides actually exchange something of value.

Then introduce the engineering frame:

"Think of a contract like an API — an interface specification for a promise. The Offer is the input the system needs. The Acceptance is the handshake that confirms both sides are connected. The Consideration is the actual data being passed."

Ask:

"If you only had the Offer, but no Acceptance, would that be a contract?" (No — there's no agreement.) "If you had Offer and Acceptance but neither side ever did anything, would that be a contract?" (Technically yes, you'd have a promise of consideration, but in practice contracts only "complete" when value is actually exchanged. For our purposes, all three must be present for the contract to fully exist.) "If you gave them the Pikachu sticker without ever asking, would that be a contract?" (No — there's no offer or acceptance. That's just a gift.)

3. The Gift vs. The Contract

This is an important distinction. Walk through it:

"If your grandparent says, 'I'll give you $20 for your birthday,' is that a contract?"

Discuss. Most students will initially say yes. But:

"A gift is when someone gives you something without expecting anything in return. A contract requires something to flow both ways. Birthday money is a gift. There's no consideration on your side. You didn't promise to do anything in return."

"But — if your grandparent said, 'I'll give you $20 if you wash the car,' THAT is a contract. Now you're promising something in return. Now it's an exchange."

Why does this matter? Because a gift can be withdrawn before it's given. A contract usually cannot. Knowing the difference matters in real life.

Guided Session 2​

Contract or Not?​

Learning Goal​

By the end of this session, the student can:

  • analyze a real-world promise and identify whether all three components are present
  • spot the missing component when a promise isn't a full contract
  • recognize contracts they've already made without realizing it

Activities​

1. The Promise Cards

Read each of these scenarios aloud. For each one, the student says CONTRACT or NOT A CONTRACT, and identifies what's missing if it's not.

Scenario 1: Your friend says, "I'll trade you my eraser for your pencil." You say "deal" and you swap. (Contract — all three parts present.)

Scenario 2: You promise yourself that you'll do your homework before screen time. (Not a contract — there's only one person involved.)

Scenario 3: Your aunt says, "I'm thinking about getting you a new bike." (Not a contract — that's a vague intention, not an offer.)

Scenario 4: Your neighbor says, "I'll pay you $5 to walk my dog." You say nothing. (Not a contract — there's an offer but no acceptance.)

Scenario 5: Your neighbor says, "I'll pay you $5 to walk my dog." You say "okay" and walk the dog. They pay you. (Contract — offer, acceptance, consideration.)

Scenario 6: You and a friend pinky-swear you'll always be best friends forever. (Not a contract — no consideration. Friendship is not legally exchangeable value.)

Scenario 7: You click "I agree" on an app's terms of service to download a game. (Contract — and a notoriously long one. The offer is the terms of service. Your "I agree" is acceptance. Your data/attention is the consideration. Yes, really.)

Scenario 8: A vending machine. You put in $1, push a button, get a soda. (Contract — the machine made an implicit offer. Your money is acceptance + consideration. The soda is the machine's consideration.)

2. Contracts You've Already Made

Together, list contracts the student has actually made in the past week. They will not have called them contracts. Examples:

  • Allowance for chores
  • Trading snacks with a friend at school
  • Agreeing to do extra dishes in exchange for a later bedtime
  • Borrowing a book with a promise to return it
  • Subscribing to anything online
  • Clicking "I agree" to play a game
  • Signing a school permission slip (technically, your parent's contract)

For each one, identify:

PromiseOfferAcceptanceConsiderationContract?

The student should be surprised by how many they've made.

3. The "Almost a Contract" Cases

These are interesting because they look like contracts but are missing one piece:

A pat on the back: "Good job, I owe you one." (Offer is too vague. What is "one"?)

A coach's pep talk: "If you all give 110% today, we'll win." (No acceptance from each player individually. No consideration the coach is offering.)

An older sibling's threat: "Don't tell mom and I'll give you my dessert." (This is a contract structurally, though it might not be enforced by a court. Discuss why — this is a great opener for next week's topics.)

Independent Practice​

Goal​

Build the habit of seeing contracts everywhere in the real world.

Activities​

1. Contract Spotter

Over one day, the student keeps a list of every contract they make or witness — including the very small ones. They should aim for at least 8 contracts spotted in 24 hours. Examples:

  • Mom said "drink your water and you can have dessert" — contract
  • Bought a snack at the store — contract
  • Signed up for an email newsletter — contract
  • Trade with a sibling — contract
  • Clicked through a game's update terms — contract

For each, write what was offered, accepted, and exchanged.

2. The Broken Contract

Find or remember one contract that wasn't fulfilled. Maybe a friend said they'd return a book and didn't. Maybe a website said they'd email a discount and didn't.

Write:

  • What the contract was (all three parts)
  • Which side broke it
  • What happened next

You don't need a perfect ending. This is just observation.

Case Notes​

Add this to your Case Notes:

Date:

One contract I made or witnessed today:

  • Offer:
  • Acceptance:
  • Consideration (both sides):

One promise that looked like a contract but wasn't — and why:

One contract that got broken — what happened next:

Sentence starters for younger learners:

  • "I made a deal where I gave ___ and got ___."
  • "A deal that wasn't really a deal was ___."

Reflection Questions​

  • Were there any contracts on your list that surprised you when you wrote them down?
  • Have you ever made a "deal" with yourself? Is that the same kind of thing as a contract?
  • What's the smallest contract you've ever made? What's the biggest?

Check for Understanding​

After this week, check whether the learner can:

  1. Name the three parts: "What are the three required parts of a contract?" (Looking for: offer, acceptance, consideration. Don't worry about word order or exact wording.)
  2. Spot a missing piece: "If your aunt says, 'I might get you a bike someday,' is that a contract? Why or why not?" (Looking for: no — there's no firm offer.)
  3. Identify a real contract: "Tell me about a contract you made this week." (Looking for: any real example with all three pieces.)

If the learner can do at least 2 of these, they're ready for Week 5.

Pause and Notice​

What Matters Here

After Session 2, ask:

"Have you ever made a promise that mattered to you a lot — but that didn't have all three parts of a contract? Like a friendship promise, or a promise to yourself?"

Sit with that. Promises that aren't legally enforceable can still be the most important promises in a person's life. The legal system handles a specific kind of promise — the ones with clear inputs, outputs, and exchanges — because those are the ones the system can actually verify and enforce. The other kind, the deeper kind, lives somewhere else entirely.

"Why do you think the law only enforces the kind of promise with a clear exchange?"

Help them land on: because the law can only handle things it can check. A pinky promise is real, but a court has no way to verify it, measure it, or fix it if broken. The legal system handles what it can handle. Everything else is up to the people involved.

This week's takeaway: A contract is one specific kind of promise — the kind with a structure clear enough that the law can step in if it breaks. Most of life's most important promises live outside that structure, and that's okay.

Spiral Review​

Connecting to Earlier Weeks

From Weeks 1–3: Last unit, we looked at rules made by a group for everyone. Now we're looking at rules made by two specific people for each other. Notice that the structure of "what makes a rule work" is the same in both cases — there has to be clarity, agreement, and consequences.

From Week 3: Several of your Three Laws probably involved promises ("everyone must do their share of chores," etc.). Many of those were really contracts in disguise.

Simplify (Ages 8–9)

Use only food, toys, and chore examples. Skip the vending machine and "I agree" scenarios — they're abstract. Focus on direct exchanges the student can act out.

Extend (Ages 10–12)

Show the student a click-through "Terms of Service" agreement on a real website. Note how long it is. Discuss: when you click "I agree" without reading it, are you actually accepting? Legally, mostly yes. Practically, almost no one reads them. What does that tell us about how the system works in practice?

Vocabulary This Week
  • Contract — A binding agreement between two or more parties, made up of an offer, an acceptance, and consideration.
  • Offer — A clear, specific statement of what one party will do in exchange for what.
  • Acceptance — A clear agreement to the offer's terms.
  • Consideration — Something of value that each side gives in exchange.
  • Binding — Required to be followed; not just a polite intention.
  • Gift — A one-way transfer of value with no consideration in return. Different from a contract.

Preview of Next Week​

Next week, we zoom in on the first two components — Offer and Acceptance. We'll learn about the "handshake protocol" of how deals actually get formed: when an offer counts as an offer, what counts as a real acceptance, and how the smallest miscommunication can prevent a contract from ever forming.
