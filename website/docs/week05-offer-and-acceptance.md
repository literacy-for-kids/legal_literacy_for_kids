# Week 5: Handshakes and How Deals Get Made
*The Anatomy of a Promise — Phase 2*

Last week, we cracked open the structure of a contract: Offer, Acceptance, Consideration. This week we zoom in on the first two — the **handshake protocol**. When does an offer actually count as an offer? When does an acceptance actually count? And what kinds of small miscommunications can prevent a contract from ever forming, even when both sides *think* they have a deal?

This is the part of contract law that handles the *connection*. Before there's any data flowing, before there's any exchange of value, two parties have to actually agree on the same thing — and that agreement has to be clear enough that a third party (like a court) could verify it later if there's a dispute.

---

:::info Facilitator Snapshot
- This week focuses on two components: Offer and Acceptance. Consideration gets its own week (Week 6).
- Pay close attention to the **counter-offer** concept. It's the source of most contract confusion in real life.
- Use lots of role-play this week — the lessons land better when the student acts out negotiations.
- The big idea: an offer is precise, an acceptance is unconditional, and anything else creates a new round of negotiation.

## For Younger Learners (Ages 8–9)

:::info Adapting This Week
**Simplest version of the concept:** "A real offer says exactly what you'll trade. A real acceptance is a simple yes. Anything else means the deal isn't done yet."

**What to shorten or skip:**
- Skip the "invitation to treat" concept — too abstract.
- Use trade-with-a-friend examples only.
- Keep sessions to 20 minutes.

**Adapting the activities:**
- Act out every offer and acceptance physically. Use props.
- Accept verbal answers.

**Journal alternative:** Draw the moment when two friends shake hands on a trade.

**What success looks like:** The learner can spot when an offer is vague and when an acceptance changes the terms.
:::

:::info For Ages 10–12
- Introduce the term **invitation to treat** — a display that looks like an offer but isn't legally one (like a price tag in a store, which is technically an invitation for *you* to make the offer at the counter).
- Discuss the concept of a **meeting of the minds** — both sides need to actually mean the same thing for the deal to be valid.
- Have them analyze advertising as offers vs. invitations to treat ("Limited time offer!" is rarely a legal offer).
:::

## Guided Session 1
### What Counts as an Offer?
#### Learning Goal
By the end of this session, the student can:

- distinguish a genuine offer from a vague intention
- identify the specific elements that make an offer "real"
- recognize advertisements and price tags as invitations to negotiate, not binding offers

---
#### Activities

**1. Real Offer vs. Not-Quite-Offer**

Read each statement aloud. Have the student decide: real offer or not?

> "I'll give you this eraser for that pencil." → **Real offer.** Specific items, clear trade.

> "I'm thinking about trading some stuff." → **Not an offer.** Too vague.

> "I might be willing to consider letting you borrow my book sometime." → **Not an offer.** Maybe, sometime, consider — none of this is concrete.

> "I'll loan you my book for the weekend if you return it Sunday by 6 PM." → **Real offer.** Specific item, specific terms.

> "Want to play later?" → **Not an offer in the contract sense.** It's an invitation to do something fun, not a deal with consideration.

> "I'll mow your lawn this Saturday for $20." → **Real offer.** Specific task, time, price.

Discuss the pattern:

> "A real offer answers three questions clearly: **what** is being exchanged, **when** or **how**, and **between whom**. If any of those is missing or vague, it's not yet a real offer."

---

**2. The Price Tag Trick**

Here's something most students find surprising:

> "When you walk into a store and see a $5 price tag on a candy bar, that price tag is *not* a legal offer. It's what lawyers call an **invitation to treat**. It's the store saying: 'We'd be interested in offers around this price.'"

> "When you take the candy bar to the counter, **you** are making the offer ('I'll pay $5 for this'). When the cashier rings you up, **they** are accepting your offer."

This sounds like a technicality, but it matters: it means a store can refuse to sell you something even if it's on the shelf. They never made an offer. They invited one.

Ask:
> "Why would the law work this way?"

The answer: stores would be in trouble if every price tag were a legal offer. Imagine if a price was mislabeled at $5 instead of $50 — the store could be forced to sell at the wrong price to every customer who saw the tag. The "invitation to treat" framing lets the store control its own offers.

---

**3. Catalog of Offers in Real Life**

Together, sort these into "real offers" vs. "invitations to treat":

| Scenario | Offer or Invitation? |
|---|---|
| Price tag in a store | Invitation |
| Online "Add to Cart" button | Invitation (your click is your offer) |
| Restaurant menu | Invitation |
| Vending machine | Real offer (the machine has pre-committed) |
| "I'll trade you this for that" | Real offer |
| Advertisement for a new game | Invitation (usually) |
| "I'll babysit Saturday for $30" | Real offer |
| Online auction listing | Invitation (your bid is your offer) |

> *Note: these distinctions can vary by country and exact wording. This is a framework, not legal advice.*

## Guided Session 2
### The Handshake Protocol
#### Learning Goal
By the end of this session, the student can:

- recognize that a real acceptance must match the offer exactly
- understand what a counter-offer does (it kills the original offer)
- play out a multi-step negotiation correctly

---
#### Activities

**1. Acceptance Must Match the Offer**

Walk through this:

> "I offer you my hat for your scarf. You say: 'Deal — but only if you also throw in your gloves.' Have we made a contract?"

The student should say no. Now explain why:

> "When you added the gloves condition, you didn't *accept* my offer. You **rejected** my offer and made a new one. That's called a **counter-offer**. The original offer is now dead — gone, vanished. You replaced it with your own offer, and now I'm the one who has to decide whether to accept *yours*."

This is one of the most important moves in contract law. **A counter-offer terminates the original offer.** Even if I want to take back what I originally said and say "fine, fine, just the hat for the scarf," that offer is no longer on the table unless I re-make it.

---

**2. Live Negotiation Role-Play**

Run a role-play. The student plays one side. You play the other.

If it helps, write each round on the [Contract Design Worksheet](./resources/contract-design-worksheet.md) so the student can see when the original offer disappears and a new one replaces it.

> **Round 1:**
> You: "I'll trade you this eraser for that pencil."
> Student: "I'll do it if you also give me a sticker."
> You: "Hmm — let me think. Actually no, I'd rather just go with the eraser-for-pencil."
> *Question:* Is your offer of "eraser-for-pencil" still valid?

Answer: technically no. The student's counter-offer killed it. You'd need to re-make the original offer for it to be alive again.

> **Round 2:**
> You: "Okay, brand new offer: I'll trade my eraser for your pencil. Yes or no?"
> Student: "Yes."
> *Now* you have a contract.

> **Round 3:**
> You: "I'll trade my eraser for your pencil."
> Student: "Okay! Can I have it tomorrow?"
> *Question:* Did the student accept?

This one's tricky. Asking a question doesn't automatically reject the offer — but it can, depending on context. If the student is asking for **clarification** ("when do you want it?"), the offer probably stays alive. If they're trying to **change the terms** ("can I have it tomorrow [instead of now]?"), that's a counter-offer.

The key insight: **a true acceptance has to match the offer exactly.** Anything else, and you're back in negotiation.

---

**3. Meeting of the Minds**

Introduce the phrase:

> "Lawyers sometimes call this a **meeting of the minds**. Both people have to actually be agreeing about the *same thing*. If you think we're trading the eraser-for-pencil and I think we're trading the eraser-for-pencil-and-sticker, we haven't had a meeting of the minds, and there's no contract yet."

This is why the small details matter. A real contract requires both sides to mean the same thing.

Ask:

> "Can you think of a time you 'made a deal' with someone but later realized you'd meant different things?"

Most students can. ("I thought you said you'd help me with one problem, not the whole worksheet!") These are real-world cases where the meeting of the minds didn't happen.

## Independent Practice
### Goal

Practice spotting whether real offers and acceptances are happening in everyday life.

### Activities

**1. The Negotiation Logbook**

Over the next few days, watch how deals get made around you. Write down at least **3 real negotiations** you witness or are part of. For each, write:

- What was the initial offer?
- Was there a counter-offer? What was it?
- Did the deal happen? If yes, what were the final terms?

Examples:
- "Mom said I could have dessert if I ate my broccoli. I said only if it's ice cream. She said okay. Deal."
- "Friend offered me their sandwich for my chips. I said only if I keep half the chips. They said no. No deal."

---

**2. The 'Almost-Accepted' Scenarios**

For each of these, write down whether a contract was formed, and if not, what would need to change:

> **A:** "I'll pay you $5 to walk my dog." "Sure, no problem!"
> **B:** "I'll pay you $5 to walk my dog." "Okay, but I want $7."
> **C:** "I'll pay you $5 to walk my dog." "Cool."
> **D:** "I'll pay you $5 to walk my dog." "Yeah, when do you want it done?"
> **E:** "I'll pay you $5 to walk my dog every weekday this month." "Just Mondays through Wednesdays?"

Answers:
- **A:** Contract formed.
- **B:** Counter-offer. No contract yet.
- **C:** Probably contract — "cool" usually reads as acceptance, though in a real dispute it could be ambiguous.
- **D:** Probably an acceptance plus a clarifying question. Contract likely formed.
- **E:** Counter-offer. The original deal is now dead.

### Reflection Questions

- Have you ever thought you had a deal, but later realized the other person meant something different? What happened?
- Are there people in your life who tend to make clear offers? Vague offers? Why does it matter?
- Why do you think most legal contracts are written down rather than just spoken?

## Check for Understanding

After this week, check whether the learner can:

1. **Recognize a real offer:** "Is 'I might trade you something later' a real offer? Why or why not?" (Looking for: not specific enough.)
2. **Explain a counter-offer:** "If I offer you a trade and you say 'yes, but only if you throw in something else,' did you accept?" (Looking for: no — that's a counter-offer, and it kills the original deal.)
3. **Spot the handshake:** "Tell me about a deal you made this week. Show me where the offer was and where the acceptance was." (Looking for: any real example with both clearly identified.)

If the learner can do at least 2 of these, they're ready for Week 6.

## Pause and Notice

:::note What Matters Here
After Session 2, ask:

> "Have you ever felt pressured to say yes to a deal before you'd really thought about it? Like someone made an offer and then expected an immediate answer?"

This is worth naming. **Pressure to accept quickly is a real-world tactic that pushes people into contracts they wouldn't otherwise sign.** It happens in advertising. It happens at car dealerships. It happens with siblings.

> "What's a good rule of thumb if someone is rushing you to accept?"

Help them land on: *you can always ask for time. The fact that someone made an offer doesn't mean you have to accept it now. An offer is an invitation to think, not a command.*

**This week's takeaway:** A real deal is two clear minds meeting on the same terms. Anything less is just talking past each other.
:::

:::tip Vocabulary This Week
- **Offer** — A clear, specific statement of what one party will exchange for what.
- **Acceptance** — A clear agreement to the offer's exact terms, without changes.
- **Counter-offer** — Changing any term of the offer. This kills the original offer and creates a new one.
- **Invitation to treat** — A display (like a price tag or menu) that looks like an offer but is actually inviting the customer to make the offer.
- **Meeting of the minds** — When both sides actually agree on the same exact terms.
:::
