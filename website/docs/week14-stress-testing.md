# Week 14: Running Your Charter Against Real Conflicts
*The Governance Project — Phase 4 Finale: Stress Testing*

You wrote a Charter last week. It looks pristine. It has rules, rights, definitions, a dispute-resolution protocol. Everyone signed it.

Now we break it.

This is the same move every serious engineer makes after writing code: **run it against weird inputs and see what crashes**. A Charter that survives only easy cases is not a real Charter — it's wishful thinking. Real foundational documents become real when they get hit with edge cases nobody anticipated and they either bend or shatter. This week, we find out which.

By the end of this week, your Charter will have **Amendment v1.1** attached to it — a documented list of patches that fix the gaps you found. That, more than the original draft, is the artifact that matters.

---

:::info Facilitator Snapshot
- This is the closing week of Unit 4. The Charter becomes a *tested* document, not just a written one.
- Run the five scenarios in order. They escalate from "small gap" to "the rules contradict each other."
- The fifth scenario is the one the Charter handles well. That balance matters — students should see what their document gets right, not only what it gets wrong.
- The output is Amendment v1.1: a real, written, dated patch list. Save it with the original Charter.
- This sets up the Justice System unit. From next week, the Charter is the governing document we apply through courts, due process, and a mock trial.
:::

## Facilitator Preparation

:::info Before You Begin
- Have the student's signed Charter from Week 13 in front of you. You will refer back to it constantly.
- Adapt the five scenarios below to fit the student's actual Charter domain. The scenarios are written generically — but they land harder when they target the student's specific Charter (their bedroom rules, their friend group rules, their Minecraft server rules).
- Have paper ready for the **Amendment Worksheet**: Scenario → Charter Provision Cited → Ruling → Gap Found → Proposed Patch.
- Plan extra time for Session 2. Drafting amendments is slower than running scenarios.
:::

:::tip Facilitation Mindset
Your role is the **adversarial tester**. Push hard on the Charter. Every time the student gives a ruling, ask: "Where in the Charter does it say that?" If they can't point to a provision, that is a gap. If they point to a provision and the wording is ambiguous, that is a gap too. Don't be mean — be precise. The Charter is the thing being tested. The student is the engineer fixing it.
:::

## For Younger Learners (Ages 8–9)

:::info Adapting This Week
**Simplest version of the concept:** "Your Charter is going to face some tricky 'what if' problems. Some it'll handle great. Some it won't. We're going to find out which is which, and then patch the holes."

**What to shorten or skip:**
- Run only 3 scenarios total instead of 5 (pick the missing rule, the ambiguous definition, and the one the Charter handles well).
- Skip the conflict-of-rules scenario — it's the hardest.
- Keep sessions to 20 minutes.

**Adapting the activities:**
- Treat each scenario as a "story problem." Read it aloud, talk it through.
- Amendments can be drawn or dictated rather than written.
- Use simple language: "missing rule," "fuzzy word," "weird situation," instead of formal terms.

**Journal alternative:** A list of "Things My Charter Forgot About" with a single fix written next to each one.

**What success looks like:** The learner can name one thing the Charter handled well and one thing it didn't, and propose at least one patch.
:::

:::info For Ages 10–12
- Have the student write a brief "case summary" for each scenario in the style of a real court opinion: Facts, Charter Provision Cited, Ruling, Reasoning.
- Push them to label each gap precisely: missing rule, ambiguous definition, conflict-of-rules, or unanticipated edge case.
- Introduce the idea of a **standing committee** — a long-term group that watches for needed amendments over time, not just in a one-off stress test.
:::

## Guided Session 1
### The First Three Scenarios
#### Learning Goal
By the end of this session, the student can:

- run a hypothetical conflict through their Charter
- identify the specific Charter provision (or absence of one) that controls the outcome
- recognize three kinds of gap: missing rule, ambiguous definition, unanticipated edge case

---
#### Activities

**1. Setting Up the Worksheet**

Hand the student a simple grid:

| Scenario | Charter Provision | My Ruling | What Kind of Gap (if any) | Proposed Patch |
|---|---|---|---|---|

Every scenario will be filled in across the row. Some rows will have "no gap" in the fourth column — that's a Charter win.

Now read this aloud:

> "We are about to stress-test the Charter. For each scenario, you are the judge. You have to: (1) find the part of the Charter that controls this case, (2) make a ruling, (3) say whether the Charter handled it well or has a gap we need to patch."

> "If you can't find a provision that controls — that's a gap. If you find one but the wording is too fuzzy to apply — that's a gap. If two provisions seem to point in different directions — that's a gap. The Charter is on trial."

---

**2. Scenario 1 — The Missing Rule**

Read aloud (adapt to the student's domain):

> "Someone in your domain does something that everyone agrees is bad — but nothing in your Charter actually forbids it. For example: a member starts a side group chat that excludes one specific member, with the explicit purpose of talking about that member behind their back. No Charter rule says 'no excluding side chats.' But it clearly violates the spirit of the document."

Have the student work the worksheet:

- Charter Provision: *(probably none — that's the lesson)*
- Ruling: What do they want to do?
- Kind of Gap: **Missing rule.**
- Proposed Patch: A new rule that captures what they want.

Key teaching move: the student will instinctively want to rule against the side-chat. Push them: "On what authority? Where in the Charter does it say that?" When they realize the Charter is silent, *that* is the lesson.

A judge can't make up rules in the moment. They can only apply what exists. If the Charter is silent, the system needs an amendment — not a creative ruling.

---

**3. Scenario 2 — The Ambiguous Definition**

Read aloud:

> "Your Charter uses the word [pick a key term from the student's Charter — 'member,' 'meeting,' 'private space,' 'group decision,' something they wrote]. A dispute comes up that hinges on what that word means. One member reads it one way; another reads it another way. Both readings are defensible."

Pick a concrete example tied to the student's Charter. If their Charter says "all members must agree on which game to play," ask: what counts as "agree"? Silence? A shrug? A reluctant "fine"?

Worksheet:
- Charter Provision: *(the rule that uses the ambiguous word)*
- Ruling: They have to pick a reading — but which one?
- Kind of Gap: **Ambiguous definition.**
- Proposed Patch: Add the term to the **Definitions** section with a precise meaning.

Connect back to Week 8 here: this is exactly the "no vehicles in the park" problem. Words are ambiguous. The Charter has to pin them down.

---

**4. Scenario 3 — The Unanticipated Edge Case**

Read aloud:

> "Something happens that nobody — not you, not anyone who signed the Charter — imagined when the Charter was written. Some kind of new situation. Maybe a new member wants to join who is not a friend or family member but a friend's sibling visiting for the weekend. Maybe a member moves away but still wants to vote in group decisions. Pick the edge case that fits your domain."

This is harder than Scenario 1, because in Scenario 1 there *should* have been a rule. In Scenario 3, the situation is genuinely new — even a perfect drafter might have missed it.

Worksheet:
- Charter Provision: Probably none, or only one that "kinda" applies.
- Ruling: They have to reason from the Charter's intent (Week 8 returns).
- Kind of Gap: **Unanticipated edge case.**
- Proposed Patch: A new rule that defines how the system handles this kind of case.

Key insight: every Charter eventually faces edge cases. The question is not whether they'll happen — they will — but how the Charter handles them. A good Charter either anticipates the edge case or gives a clear procedure for ruling on novel ones.

## Guided Session 2
### The Last Two Scenarios and Drafting Amendments
#### Learning Goal
By the end of this session, the student can:

- identify when two Charter provisions conflict with each other
- recognize a scenario the Charter handles well
- draft amendments in formal language ready for ratification

---
#### Activities

**1. Scenario 4 — The Conflict of Rules**

This one is the hardest. Read aloud:

> "Two parts of your Charter both apply to the same situation, but they point in different directions. For example: your Charter says 'every member has the right to be heard before a decision is made about them' AND 'group decisions are made within 24 hours to prevent endless arguments.' What happens when the member who needs to be heard is unavailable for 24 hours?"

Adapt this to the student's actual Charter. Look for two provisions that *could* end up in tension — almost every Charter has at least one such pair.

Worksheet:
- Charter Provision: **Two provisions, both apply.**
- Ruling: The student has to decide which one wins, and on what principle.
- Kind of Gap: **Conflict-of-rules.**
- Proposed Patch: Add an explicit **priority rule** — when these two clauses conflict, which one wins? Many real constitutions have these. (Example: in the US, rights typically beat ordinary laws when they collide.)

This is the deepest scenario in the set. It teaches that even a well-written document can contain internal contradictions, and that good legal systems pre-decide which rules take priority over others.

---

**2. Scenario 5 — The Win**

After four scenarios of finding gaps, run one where the Charter does its job. Read aloud:

> "A member breaks one of the explicit rules in your Charter. The rule is clear. The violation is clear. The dispute-resolution protocol says exactly what happens. Walk through what the Charter does."

This scenario should pull the student through the Charter the way it was designed to be used:
- The rule was violated.
- The dispute-resolution procedure is triggered.
- Someone (per the Charter) hears the case.
- A ruling is made.
- A remedy is applied.

Worksheet:
- Charter Provision: A clear, well-drafted rule plus the dispute-resolution protocol.
- Ruling: Smooth and well-supported.
- Kind of Gap: **None.** The Charter worked.
- Proposed Patch: None needed.

> "Notice how it feels when the Charter works. The argument doesn't go in circles. The procedure is clear. The remedy fits the violation. That is what a good rule looks like in action — and your Charter has at least one of those built in."

---

**3. Drafting Amendment v1.1**

Now collect everything. Across the four gap scenarios, the student has four proposed patches. Time to write them up formally.

Use the **Amendment Template** introduced in Week 13:

```
═══════════════════════════════════════════════
AMENDMENT v1.1 to THE [NAME] CHARTER
Proposed by: [Student's name]
Date: ___________
═══════════════════════════════════════════════

AMENDMENT 1 — [Short title, e.g., "No Exclusion Chats"]
Type: New rule
Text: [The new rule, written precisely]
Purpose: [Why we're adding it — what gap it patches]
Triggered by: Scenario 1 stress test

AMENDMENT 2 — [Short title, e.g., "Definition of 'Agreement'"]
Type: New definition
Text: ["Agreement" means ...]
Purpose: To prevent ambiguity in [Rule X]
Triggered by: Scenario 2 stress test

AMENDMENT 3 — [Short title]
Type: New rule
Text: [...]
Purpose: To cover edge cases like [...]
Triggered by: Scenario 3 stress test

AMENDMENT 4 — [Short title, e.g., "Priority Rule for Rights vs. Speed"]
Type: Priority rule
Text: "When the right to be heard conflicts with the 24-hour decision rule, the right to be heard wins. The 24-hour clock pauses until the member is reachable."
Purpose: To resolve the conflict-of-rules between Rights Section and Decision Section
Triggered by: Scenario 4 stress test
```

The amendment list doesn't need to be ratified in this session. It just needs to exist on paper.

## Independent Practice
### Goal

Finalize the amendment list, get the other members to ratify it, and observe the Charter operating in its v1.1 form.

### Activities

**1. The Ratification**

The Charter said how amendments get adopted. Follow that procedure. Present Amendment v1.1 to the other members of the domain. Some of your amendments may get pushback. Some may need to be revised before ratification. That's exactly how it's supposed to work.

Note what happens:
- Which amendments passed without changes?
- Which amendments got modified during ratification?
- Were any amendments rejected? Why?

---

**2. The Updated Charter**

Once Amendment v1.1 is ratified, write a clean copy of the **updated Charter** — original text plus amendments — so that there is one document that reflects the current rules.

Real legal systems do this too. The US Constitution exists as the original text plus 27 amendments. The full current legal effect is the combination.

---

**3. Watch It Run**

For a few days, the new Charter v1.1 is in effect. Watch for:
- Did the amendments solve the problems they were designed to solve?
- Did any of the amendments accidentally create new problems?
- Are there *new* gaps you didn't see during the stress test?

Note any new gaps for a future Amendment v1.2. (Real legal systems are constantly being amended.)

## Check for Understanding

After this week, check whether the learner can:

1. **Name the four kinds of gap:** "What are the different ways a Charter can fail in a stress test?" (Looking for: missing rule, ambiguous definition, unanticipated edge case, conflict-of-rules.)
2. **Show v1.1:** "Can I see Amendment v1.1?" (Looking for: a real written amendment list, ratified or in the process of being ratified.)
3. **Defend a patch:** "Tell me about one amendment you wrote and why." (Looking for: a clear link between a stress-test scenario, the gap it revealed, and the patch.)

If the learner can do at least 2 of these, they're ready for Unit 5.

## Pause and Notice

:::note What Matters Here
After ratifying Amendment v1.1, ask:

> "Was it weirder to write the Charter, or weirder to amend it?"

Most students find amending stranger than drafting. Writing felt like creating something new. Amending felt like admitting the original was incomplete.

> "Real legal systems are mostly running on amendments. The original US Constitution from 1787 is still in there, but a huge amount of how the country actually works comes from amendments and later interpretations. Foundational documents are starting points, not finished products."

> "Every system that has ever worked has been patched. Your Charter is now closer to a real one than it was last week — because real ones have v1.1s."

**This week's takeaway:** A document that has been stress-tested and amended is more trustworthy than a pristine document that has never been challenged. The patches are not flaws. They are evidence that the system is paying attention.
:::

## Spiral Review

:::tip Connecting to Earlier Weeks
- **From Week 8 (Letter vs. Intent):** The ambiguous-definition scenario is a Letter-vs-Intent case in miniature. When the words don't tell you what to do, you have to ask what the rule was *for*.
- **From Week 9 (Loopholes):** The missing-rule scenario is a loophole-shaped gap — there's no patch yet, so a clever member could exploit the silence.
- **From Week 10 (Precedent):** When a Charter is silent on an edge case, your ruling on it sets a precedent for similar cases later. Treat that ruling carefully.
- **From Week 13:** The original Charter is the artifact being tested. Everything you wrote last week now meets reality.
:::

---

:::tip Simplify (Ages 8–9)
Run only Scenarios 1, 2, and 5. The lesson — *"every rule has gaps, and a good Charter gets fixed when gaps show up"* — comes through with three scenarios just as well as five. Patches can be drawn or dictated.
:::

:::tip Extend (Ages 10–12)
After ratifying Amendment v1.1, have the older learner write a one-page "Charter Maintenance Plan": how often will the Charter be reviewed? Under what conditions does a stress-test get triggered? Who has authority to call for an amendment? Real organizations have these.
:::

:::tip Vocabulary This Week
- **Stress test** — Deliberately running a system against hard cases to find where it breaks.
- **Gap** — A place where the Charter is silent, ambiguous, or contradictory.
- **Conflict-of-rules** — When two parts of the same document point in different directions and both apply.
- **Priority rule** — A rule that decides which other rule wins when two collide.
- **Amendment v1.1** — The first patch release of a Charter, after it has met reality.
:::
