# Optional Week 2: Rules Between Nations
*Extension — Beyond the Core Curriculum*

So far in this curriculum, every legal system has had something the system above it could lean on:

- A household has parents who enforce rules.
- A school has staff who enforce rules.
- A country has police and courts that enforce rules.

What happens when you reach the top of the stack?

**Nations.** There is no government over the governments. There is no global police force, no planet-level court with the power to drag a country to trial. And yet — *somehow* — there is something called **international law**, and most of the time, most countries follow most of it. How?

This week, we look at the strangest layer of the legal system: the layer where no one is in charge, and the rules still mostly work.

---

:::info Facilitator Snapshot
- This is an optional extension week. It is the most abstract week in the curriculum and works best for older learners or as a discussion-heavy session.
- The big move: international law is the cleanest case of "rules without a top-level enforcer." Understanding *why it works at all* is the most interesting question.
- The week ties cleanly to Week 11 (rights as firewalls — here, sovereignty acts as a national-level firewall) and Week 4 (contracts — treaties are state-level contracts).
- This week's activity is a drafted "household treaty" — a contract between members of the household with no third-party enforcer, modeling the international situation in miniature.

## Facilitator Preparation

:::info Before You Begin
- Have one example of a real treaty in mind (the Paris Climate Agreement, a trade deal, the Geneva Conventions). You don't need details — just a name to anchor "this is what a treaty looks like."
- Skim the basics of the UN: founded 1945, has a Security Council, has a General Assembly, has the International Court of Justice, and does not have a standing army or police force.
- Decide whether you want to connect this week to the Environmental Literacy curriculum's discussion of international agreements (Paris Accord, etc.). It's a nice cross-link if your student has done that track.
:::

:::tip Facilitation Mindset
This week is more conversation than activity. Be willing to sit with the strangeness of the topic. "How does international law work?" is a question that genuinely confuses experts. The answer is partial and probabilistic — it works because of reputation, reciprocity, and consequences, *not* because anyone has the power to compel compliance. Let the student feel the weirdness.
:::

## For Younger Learners (Ages 8–9)

:::info Adapting This Week
**Simplest version of the concept:** "There are rules between countries, but no parent or principal to enforce them. So how do they work? The same way it works when nobody is watching: because you care what other people think of you, and because if you break a promise, no one will trust you next time."

**What to shorten or skip:**
- Skip the UN structure and sovereignty discussion. Just teach: "Countries agree on rules, and they mostly follow them — because if they don't, other countries don't want to deal with them anymore."
- Skip the household treaty drafting; use a verbal example instead.
- Keep sessions to 20 minutes.

**Adapting the activities:**
- Use a friend-group analogy: how do rules work in a friend group where there's no parent? The same way.

**Journal alternative:** A drawing of two countries with a piece of paper labeled "treaty" between them, and a line of small drawings showing what happens if one country breaks it (other countries stop trading, stop visiting, etc.).

**What success looks like:** The learner can give one reason countries follow international rules even though nobody can force them to.
:::

:::info For Ages 10–12
- Discuss the difference between **hard law** (treaties with binding obligations) and **soft law** (declarations and customs that shape behavior without strict enforcement).
- Introduce **the Security Council veto** as a structural feature of the UN — five permanent members can each block major actions. Why was the UN designed that way? (To get the major powers to participate at all.)
- Explore the tension between **international human rights law** and **national sovereignty**: when does the international community have the right to intervene in what a country does to its own citizens? This is one of the genuinely unsettled questions of the modern era.
:::

## Guided Session 1
### How Rules Work When No One Is In Charge
#### Learning Goal
By the end of this session, the student can:

- describe what international law is and what makes it structurally different from national law
- explain why countries follow international rules despite the absence of an enforcer
- identify treaties as a kind of contract between states

---
#### Activities

**1. The Stack of Systems**

Draw a layered diagram:

```
┌─────────────────────────────────┐
│   INTERNATIONAL LAW             │ ← no enforcer above this
│   (between nations)             │
└─────────────────────────────────┘
              ↑
┌─────────────────────────────────┐
│   NATIONAL LAW                  │ ← enforced by the state
│   (within a country)            │
└─────────────────────────────────┘
              ↑
┌─────────────────────────────────┐
│   INSTITUTIONAL RULES           │ ← enforced by schools,
│   (school, work, etc.)          │   workplaces, etc.
└─────────────────────────────────┘
              ↑
┌─────────────────────────────────┐
│   HOUSEHOLD RULES               │ ← enforced by family
│                                 │
└─────────────────────────────────┘
```

Walk up the stack with the student:

> "At each level, there's someone above who can enforce. Parents enforce household rules. Schools enforce institutional rules. The state enforces national law. But what enforces international law? **Nothing — no level above it exists.**"

> "And yet, international law is real. Treaties get signed. Trade deals get honored. Wars are governed by rules (mostly). Most of the time, countries actually follow the rules they've agreed to. How can that be?"

---

**2. Treaties as Contracts Between States**

Define it:

> "A **treaty** is the international version of a contract. The structure is identical to what we learned in Unit 2 of this curriculum: **offer, acceptance, consideration**."

Walk through it:

| Contract element | Treaty version |
|---|---|
| **Offer** | One country proposes the terms of an agreement. |
| **Acceptance** | The other country agrees to the terms, usually formally ratified by its government. |
| **Consideration** | Each country gives something the other one wants: trade access, military cooperation, peace, emission reductions, financial aid. |

> "If a treaty has all three, it is — in the formal sense — a binding agreement. Both countries are 'on the hook' for what they promised. Just like a contract."

Examples (don't get bogged down in detail):

- **Trade treaties:** "We'll let your goods into our country without taxing them, if you let our goods into yours."
- **Defense treaties:** "If anyone attacks you, we'll help defend you. And vice versa."
- **Environmental treaties (like the Paris Agreement):** "We'll all aim to reduce emissions by [target], because climate change affects everyone."
- **Human rights treaties:** "We agree that our citizens have these protections, and we'll be accountable for upholding them."

---

**3. The Big Question: Why Do They Work?**

Now sit with the puzzle:

> "Here's the strange thing. If a country breaks a treaty, nobody can show up and arrest them. There's no global jail. So why do most countries follow most treaties most of the time? What's holding them in?"

Let the student brainstorm. Help them work toward these three answers:

**Reason 1 — Reciprocity.** "If you break the deal with me, I'll break the deal with you. If you stop letting my goods through, I'll stop letting yours through. Cooperation only works if both sides keep their end."

**Reason 2 — Reputation.** "If a country is known for breaking its treaties, nobody will sign new ones with them. Reputation is the long-term currency of international relations."

**Reason 3 — Economic consequences.** "Other countries can impose **sanctions** — meaning, they restrict trade or freeze assets — as a way of pressuring the rule-breaker. Without violence, but with real economic pain."

> "Notice what's missing from this list: *direct force*. International law isn't backed by police. It's backed by the long-term self-interest of countries who want to keep being able to cooperate."

---

**4. The Comparison**

Land it:

> "International law works the way a friend group works. There's no parent enforcing it. But if you break a friend's trust, the other friends notice. If you keep breaking trust, eventually no one wants to hang out with you. The cost of being known as untrustworthy is real, even when there's no formal punishment."

> "The international community is the largest, slowest-moving friend group in the world."

## Guided Session 2
### Sovereignty, the UN, and the Limits
#### Learning Goal
By the end of this session, the student can:

- define sovereignty as a national-level firewall against outside jurisdiction
- describe what the UN can and can't do
- identify the genuine tension between international human rights law and national sovereignty

---
#### Activities

**1. Sovereignty as a Firewall**

Define it:

> "**Sovereignty** is the principle that each nation's internal law is its own business. What happens inside a country's borders is, generally, that country's decision. Sovereignty is what stops one nation from being able to make rules for another nation."

Connect it back to Week 11:

> "Remember rights as firewalls? Sovereignty is the country-level version of the same idea. A right is a firewall protecting an individual from institutional power. Sovereignty is a firewall protecting a nation from outside power."

> "This is one of the most important design principles of the international system. It means no country can be casually overruled by other countries on internal matters. It's why the UN can't just *order* a country to change its laws."

---

**2. The Big Tension: Sovereignty vs. Human Rights**

Now name the genuine conflict:

> "Here's where it gets hard. International human rights law says: every person, in every country, has certain protections. But sovereignty says: what happens inside a country is that country's business."

> "What happens when those collide?"

If a government is genuinely mistreating its own citizens, do other countries have the right to intervene? Or does sovereignty mean the international community has to let it happen?

There is no clean answer. The international community has, at different times:

- Intervened militarily (sometimes successfully, sometimes disastrously)
- Imposed sanctions to pressure change
- Issued declarations of condemnation that did nothing
- Ignored the situation entirely

> "Reasonable people disagree about when intervention is justified. The tension between sovereignty and human rights is one of the genuinely unsolved problems in modern international law. We're not going to settle it. But you should know it exists."

For younger learners, keep this brief. For older learners, this can be a deeper discussion.

---

**3. The UN as a Governance System**

Briefly explain what the UN is and isn't:

| The UN does have | The UN does not have |
|---|---|
| A forum where every country has a voice (General Assembly) | A standing army |
| A Security Council that can authorize sanctions and (rarely) military action | The power to force a country to comply |
| Specialized agencies (World Health Organization, UNICEF, etc.) | Direct authority over any nation's internal laws |
| The International Court of Justice (ICJ), which handles disputes between states | A police force |
| Significant moral and economic influence | Direct enforcement power |

> "The UN is not a world government. It's more like a **shared meeting room** where countries can negotiate, declare positions, and coordinate. Its real power is convening — getting people in the room — not commanding."

The **Security Council** is the closest thing the UN has to real teeth. Its five permanent members (US, UK, France, Russia, China) each have a **veto** — meaning any one of them can block major action. This is structural: it was the price of getting those countries to participate at all. It also means the Security Council often can't act when its permanent members disagree.

---

**4. Connecting to the Environmental Curriculum**

If your student has done Environmental Literacy, draw the connection:

> "Remember the discussion of the Paris Agreement and climate treaties? Those are international law. The reason compliance with climate agreements is so uneven — some countries hit their targets, some don't, and there's no real consequence for missing them — is because the enforcement mechanism is just reciprocity, reputation, and economic pressure. There's no global authority that can *make* a country reduce emissions. That's why these agreements are hard to enforce, even when most countries agree they should be honored."

> "It's also why the climate problem is structurally hard. The atmosphere is a global commons (Week 2 of this curriculum!). The polluters can free-ride on the cleanup done by everyone else. And the system at the top — international law — has no top-level enforcer to fix the free-rider problem."

## Independent Practice
### Goal

Apply the international-law mindset to a real treaty in miniature — drafted between members of the student's own household.

### Activities

**1. The Household Treaty**

Pick a real shared resource or coordination point with another member of the household. Examples:

- Use of a shared TV or game console
- Use of a bathroom in the morning
- Use of shared food (snacks, a favorite drink)
- Use of a shared workspace or table

Now draft a **treaty** — not a Charter, just a bilateral agreement — between you and one other household member, covering this shared resource.

The treaty must have:

```
═══════════════════════════════════════════════
TREATY BETWEEN [Your name] AND [Other party]
Re: [Shared resource]
Date: ___________
═══════════════════════════════════════════════

OFFER:
[What you are offering to do]

ACCEPTANCE:
[What the other party agrees to in exchange]

CONSIDERATION:
[The value flowing in each direction]

TERMS:
1. [Specific provision]
2. [Specific provision]
3. [Specific provision]

ENFORCEMENT MECHANISM:
[How will you handle a violation?
There is no parent-level enforcer for this treaty —
you have to design the enforcement yourselves, like
international law. What's the consequence?]

REVIEW:
This treaty will be reviewed on [date / under what
conditions].

Signed:
________________________  ________________________
[Your name]                [Other party]
```

The trickiest part is the **enforcement mechanism**. Most household treaties between siblings have no real enforcement — which makes them exactly like international law. You have to fall back on reciprocity ("if you don't do your part, I won't do mine"), reputation ("you'll be known as untrustworthy"), or escalation ("we'll have to bring this to a parent" — that's like the Security Council).

---

**2. Watch the Treaty Run**

For a few days, the treaty is in effect. Observe:

- Did both sides honor it?
- Was there a near-violation? How was it handled?
- What kept it working — the writing? The reciprocity? Both?
- Did you ever consider escalating to a third party? Why or why not?

This is the lived experience of international law in miniature.

## Check for Understanding

After this week, check whether the learner can:

1. **Define international law:** "What is international law, and what makes it different from national law?" (Looking for: rules between countries, no top-level enforcer, works through reciprocity / reputation / sanctions.)
2. **Define sovereignty:** "What is sovereignty?" (Looking for: each nation's internal law is its own business; firewall against outside power.)
3. **Identify the tension:** "What's the conflict between international human rights law and sovereignty?" (Looking for: the international community wants to protect individuals everywhere, but sovereignty says each country handles its own internal matters.)

If the learner can do 2 of 3, they've absorbed the structural lesson.

## Pause and Notice

:::note What Matters Here
After the household treaty exercise, ask:

> "What's the strangest thing about international law to you?"

Many students land on: *that it works at all.* When you really sit with the fact that there's no police force enforcing the rules between countries, and yet most countries follow most rules most of the time, the system starts to look surprisingly stable for something with no enforcer.

> "What you're noticing is something deep: rules don't only work because someone enforces them. They also work because people *want them to work*. Cooperation has a value all its own. A world where treaties were broken constantly would be a much worse world to live in, even for the country breaking them. So most countries — most of the time — choose to honor their commitments because the long game is more valuable than any individual short-term win."

> "That principle applies far beyond international law. It's true in friend groups. It's true in marriages. It's true in business. Most of the cooperation in the world is *not* enforced from above. It's sustained from within, because people benefit from being trustworthy."

**This week's takeaway:** Rules can work without a top-level enforcer. Reciprocity, reputation, and consequences are real forces — strong enough to hold up an entire layer of the legal system. It is also the *weakest* layer, where compliance is most uneven and where the gap between what was promised and what actually happens can be widest. Both things are true at once.
:::

:::tip Vocabulary This Week
- **International law** — The body of rules between nations. No top-level enforcer.
- **Treaty** — A formal agreement between two or more states. The international version of a contract.
- **Sovereignty** — The principle that each nation's internal law is its own business. A national-level firewall.
- **Reciprocity** — "I'll keep my end if you keep yours." One of the main mechanisms holding international law together.
- **Sanctions** — Economic pressure imposed by one country (or group of countries) on another to punish a violation or compel a change in behavior.
- **UN (United Nations)** — A global forum where countries negotiate, declare positions, and coordinate. Not a world government.
:::

A final reminder: like the rest of this curriculum, this week is an educational systems toolkit, not legal advice. International law is one of the most complex specialties in the entire legal world. The point of this week is the structural framing — *rules without a top-level enforcer* — not coverage of doctrine. If you ever need real international legal expertise, talk to someone who specializes in it.
