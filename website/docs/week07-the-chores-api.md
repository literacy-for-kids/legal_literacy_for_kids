# Week 7: Writing an Airtight Agreement
*The Anatomy of a Promise — Phase 2 Finale: The Chores API*

This week, you're going to write a real household agreement.

Not a pretend contract. Not a worksheet that gets forgotten. A **real, signed document** that you and someone in your house will actually use and review. We're going to call it the **Chores API**, because that's what you're really writing: an interface specification for a household task, with inputs, outputs, edge cases, and remedies when things go off track.

By the end of this week, you'll have a piece of paper signed by you and a parent or sibling that defines exactly what "cleaning the kitchen" (or whatever task you choose) means, what each side is providing, what counts as a missed obligation, and what happens next. This is the practical capstone of Unit 2.

---

:::info Facilitator Snapshot
- This is the most hands-on, *real* lesson in the curriculum so far. The student walks away with an actual signed document they can use and revise.
- The parent or other party who signs the contract has to be on board with following it. Discuss in advance.
- The exercise is more important than the actual chore. Even if the agreement is for a small thing (feeding the dog, taking out the trash), the process of drafting it is what matters.
- This is a practice agreement, not a promise that a court would enforce the arrangement between a child and a family member.
- Expect some pushback in the drafting process. Real contracts are negotiated. That's a feature, not a bug.
:::

:::note Scope Note
Many household agreements between minors and family members are not treated the same way as business contracts in court. We still use contract-design tools here because they teach clarity, reciprocity, notice, and revision.
:::

## Facilitator Preparation

:::info Before You Begin
- **Pick the task in advance.** You and the student should agree on what household task the agreement will cover. Common choices: kitchen cleanup, dog walking, trash duty, laundry folding, dishes after dinner, bed-making, weekly room cleaning.
- **Be willing to actually use the agreement.** This means the parent/co-signer has to follow it too. If you back out after it is signed, the lesson collapses.
- Use the [Contract Design Worksheet](./resources/contract-design-worksheet.md) for drafting and the [Chores API / Household Agreement Template](./resources/chores-api-household-agreement-template.md) for the final version.
- Keep the agreement low-stakes. Avoid humiliating penalties, money pressure, or anything that would make the exercise feel threatening.
- Read both Sessions so you can guide drafting without dominating.
- Have a clean piece of paper ready for the final signed document.
:::

:::tip Facilitation Mindset
This week, your job is to **push the student into specificity**. When they write "I'll clean the kitchen," ask "what does 'clean' mean exactly?" When they write "after dinner," ask "by what time?" Every vague phrase is a future argument. The whole point is to flush out the vague parts in advance.
:::

## For Younger Learners (Ages 8–9)

:::info Adapting This Week
**Simplest version of the concept:** "Real contracts spell out exactly what each person will do. The clearer it is, the less arguing there'll be later."

**What to shorten or skip:**
- Pick a small, simple task (feeding a pet, setting the table) rather than a complex one (full kitchen cleanup).
- Skip the "remedies for breach" section — just write what happens if the task isn't done.
- Keep sessions to 20 minutes.

**Adapting the activities:**
- The contract can be in pictures or pictures-plus-short-sentences.
- Read aloud and discuss orally. The student doesn't have to write everything.

**Journal alternative:** Draw the contract as a comic strip. "If I do this, then I get this. If I don't, then this happens."

**What success looks like:** The learner has a signed (or stickered, or initialed) piece of paper specifying what they'll do and what they'll get in return.
:::

:::info For Ages 10–12
- Push the student to think through at least **three edge cases** ("what if I'm sick?", "what if the dishwasher is broken?", "what if there's a holiday?").
- Introduce the formal agreement structure: Parties, Purpose, Obligations, Edge Cases, Breach, Remedies, Review, Signatures.
- Encourage them to write a realistic **response-to-breach clause** (for example: reschedule, make-up task, pause and renegotiate, or a clearly defined consequence tied to the agreement).
:::

## Guided Session 1
### Specifying the Service
#### Learning Goal
By the end of this session, the student can:

- precisely define what the task actually involves (the "service specification")
- identify the inputs needed and the outputs expected
- recognize where vague language creates future disputes

---
#### Activities

**1. Pick the Task and Define It**

The student names the task they want to contract for. Common choices: kitchen cleanup, dog walking, laundry, dishes.

Now, push on the definition:

> "Tell me exactly what 'cleaning the kitchen' means. Like — if I were a robot who had never seen a kitchen, what would I need to do?"

Have the student list every component:

- All dishes washed and put in the dishwasher
- All countertops wiped down
- The sink is clean and empty
- The floor is swept
- The garbage is taken out IF it's more than 3/4 full
- The lights are off when finished

This list is the **service specification**. It transforms "cleaning the kitchen" from a vague phrase into a checklist that both parties can verify.

---

**2. Define Inputs and Outputs**

Borrow from engineering. Ask:

> "What inputs do you need to do this task? And what's the output — what does the kitchen look like when you're done?"

| Inputs (what you need) | Outputs (what's delivered) |
|---|---|
| Working dishwasher | Clean countertops |
| Sponge and soap | Empty sink |
| Garbage bags | Swept floor |
| Broom | Lights off |
| About 20 minutes | Garbage taken out (conditional) |

This matters because if an input is missing, the task can't be done. We'll handle that in Session 2 (edge cases).

---

**3. Specify the Consideration**

Now the other side. What does the student get in exchange?

This is where negotiation happens. In this curriculum, consideration means what each side is promising or giving in the agreement, even if a household agreement would not be treated exactly like a business contract in court. Common consideration:
- A specific amount of money (allowance bump)
- A specific privilege (extra screen time, later bedtime on weekends)
- A specific permission (sleepover, special outing)

The consideration must be **specific**. "Allowance" isn't specific — "$5 added to weekly allowance" is. "More screen time" isn't specific — "30 extra minutes on Saturdays" is.

Write up the basic contract so far:

> **Party A (the student)** will: [list of obligations from the service specification]
> **Party B (the parent)** will: [the specific consideration]
> **Frequency:** [daily, weekly, before what time, etc.]

## Guided Session 2
### Edge Cases and Breach
#### Learning Goal
By the end of this session, the student can:

- identify what could go wrong with the contract
- write rules for how each edge case is handled
- specify what counts as breach and what the remedy is

---
#### Activities

**1. Edge Case Brainstorm**

Ask:

> "What could go wrong with this contract? Imagine you're trying to find every possible weird situation."

Help them brainstorm:

- The student is sick.
- The dishwasher is broken.
- The student is away (visiting grandparents, on vacation).
- There's a family event that runs late and the kitchen isn't usable until morning.
- The kitchen was already clean (no work needed).
- Someone else used the kitchen after the student "finished" and made it messy.
- The student finished but didn't do it well.
- The parent forgot to provide the allowance / consideration on time.

For each edge case, the student writes a clause for the contract:

> **If the student is sick** — the task is rescheduled, not skipped. They make it up the following day.
> **If equipment is broken** — the obligation pauses until the equipment is fixed. No breach.
> **If someone else messes up the kitchen after completion** — the task is considered done; cleanup of the new mess is not the student's responsibility.
> **If the student did the task poorly** — Party B (parent) must point out the specific problem within 1 hour, and the student must fix it. After that hour, the work is accepted.

---

**2. Breach and Remedies**

Now the serious part. What happens if either side just *doesn't follow through*?

In contract law, breaking your obligation is called **breach**. The agreed-upon response is called the **remedy**.

For this contract, the student writes:

> **Breach by Party A (student):**
> - If the task is not done by the agreed-upon time, with no valid edge case, then:
>   - First time: a reminder and a same-day make-up plan.
>   - Repeated misses: the consideration for that task cycle may pause until the work is completed or the agreement is renegotiated.
>   - If the pattern continues: both sides review whether the agreement is realistic and fair.

> **Breach by Party B (parent):**
> - If the consideration is not provided by the agreed-upon time:
>   - Party B provides it at the next agreed review time.
>   - Both sides discuss what caused the miss.
>   - If repeated misses happen, either side may pause and renegotiate the agreement.

Notice: a fair agreement specifies what happens for **both sides** when they miss their obligations. If the document only punishes the student and never names the other side's duties, it is not well-designed.

---

**3. Draft, Sign, and Display**

Write the final contract on a clean sheet of paper. Use this structure:

```
═══════════════════════════════════════════════
THE CHORES API
A Household Agreement for a Shared Task
═══════════════════════════════════════════════

PARTIES
- Party A: [Student's name]
- Party B: [Parent or other co-signer's name]
- Effective Date: [today's date]
- Review Date: [4 weeks from today]

THE TASK
[Specific service specification — all components]

INPUTS REQUIRED
[Tools, supplies, time]

CONSIDERATION
- Party A provides: [the task, by [time/frequency]]
- Party B provides: [the specific compensation/privilege, by [time/frequency]]

EDGE CASES
- If [sickness]: [rule]
- If [broken equipment]: [rule]
- If [absence]: [rule]
- If [late completion]: [rule]
- [Add others as needed]

BREACH AND REMEDIES
- Breach by Party A: [consequences]
- Breach by Party B: [consequences]

AMENDMENT CLAUSE
- This contract may be re-negotiated by mutual agreement at any time, and must be reviewed on the Review Date above.

SIGNATURES
_________________________     _________________________
Party A                       Party B
Date:                         Date:
```

Both parties sign. Display the contract somewhere visible (refrigerator, bedroom door).

**This is a real document.** Use it. Update it when reality breaks it. That's the whole point.

## Independent Practice
### Goal

Run the contract for real and start noticing what works and what doesn't.

### Activities

**1. Run the Contract for a Week**

The student executes the contract for the full week. Both parties follow it as written.

Each day, the student notes:
- Did the task get done?
- Did the consideration get paid?
- Were any edge cases triggered?
- Were there any "almost-disputes" that the contract resolved before they escalated?

---

**2. Note the Gaps**

By the end of the week, the student should have at least **one situation the contract didn't perfectly anticipate**. Maybe someone bent the rules. Maybe an edge case showed up that wasn't covered. Maybe the consideration didn't feel fair anymore.

Write it down. We will use these gaps when we get to interpretation (Weeks 8–10) and the Micro-Charter (Weeks 13–14).

### Case Notes

Add this to your **Case Notes**:

> **Date:**
>
> **My signed Chores API — task summary:**
>
> **The consideration each side provides:**
>
> **One edge case the contract handled well:**
>
> **One situation the contract didn't fully cover — what happened?**
>
> **One change I would make in version 2.0:**

**Sentence starters for younger learners:**
- "My job in the contract is ___."
- "The other person's job is ___."
- "If I don't do it, ___ happens."

### Reflection Questions

- Did writing the contract feel like a bigger conversation than usual chore-negotiation? Why?
- Was there any moment when you wanted to "skip" the contract and just do things the old way? What was happening then?
- If you wrote contracts for everything in your life, would that make life better or worse? Why?

## Check for Understanding

After this week, check whether the learner can:

1. **Show the contract:** "Can I see your Chores API?" (Looking for: a real, signed document — even imperfectly written.)
2. **Identify a breach clause:** "What happens if you don't do your task one day?" (Looking for: a specific consequence that was written in advance.)
3. **Spot a gap:** "What's one thing your contract doesn't handle well?" (Looking for: any honest answer — naming a gap shows real understanding.)

If the learner can do at least 2 of these, they're ready for Week 8.

## Pause and Notice

:::note What Matters Here
After running the contract for a few days, ask:

> "Did anything about having a written contract change the *feel* of doing the chore? Did it feel more like a job, less like a chore? More fair, less personal? Something else?"

Real contracts are interesting objects. They make obligations clearer — which is good for predictability — but they can also make a relationship feel more transactional. Both effects are real.

> "Adults often face this exact trade-off. Should this be written down, or should we just trust each other? Writing it down is clearer; not writing it down keeps the relationship warmer. Both choices have costs."

Don't try to resolve this. Just let the student notice it.

**This week's takeaway:** A well-written contract isn't a sign of distrust — it's a tool for preventing disputes that haven't happened yet. The clearer the contract, the less arguing there is later. But the legal frame is one frame. Not every part of a relationship belongs inside it.

> *This is a thought experiment and educational exercise, not legal advice. Real household agreements between minors and parents are not generally enforceable in court — but the practice of writing one is great training for the kind of careful thinking that real-world contracts require.*
:::

## Spiral Review

:::tip Connecting to Earlier Weeks
**From Week 4:** Your contract has all three required parts (offer, acceptance, consideration). Find them in your signed document.

**From Week 5:** Negotiating the contract probably involved counter-offers ("$5 is too low — make it $7"). Notice how the back-and-forth eventually landed on a meeting of the minds.

**From Week 6:** Your consideration was specific and flowed both ways. Notice that without specifying consideration on both sides, the document would have been a list of one person's chores — not a contract.
:::

---

:::tip Simplify (Ages 8–9)
Use a simple template: "I will do [task]. You will give me [consideration]. If I don't do [task], then [simple consequence]. If you don't give me [consideration], then [simple consequence]." Skip the formal sections.
:::

:::tip Extend (Ages 10–12)
Have older learners write **two** contracts — one for themselves (a household chore) and one between two siblings (e.g., a contract for sharing the TV). Compare the two: how does the structure shift when neither party has authority over the other?
:::

:::tip Vocabulary This Week
- **Specification** — A precise description of what is being done or provided. The more specific, the fewer disputes.
- **Edge case** — An unusual situation the basic rule doesn't quite handle.
- **Breach** — Failing to follow through on a contractual obligation.
- **Remedy** — The agreed-upon consequence when a breach happens.
- **Amendment clause** — The part of a contract that says how it can be changed in the future.
:::
